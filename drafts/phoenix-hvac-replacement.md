In Phoenix, a failed air conditioner is not a minor inconvenience. A summer outage can become a health and safety problem, and that urgency creates a perfect opening for vague scopes, oversized equipment, rushed financing, and “today only” discounts.

Arizona gives homeowners useful protections, but only if the contractor is properly licensed and the contract is detailed. The Arizona Registrar of Contractors warns consumers to verify the license, confirm that the license classification matches the work, get at least three written estimates, and spell out who obtains permits.

## 1. Diagnose before replacing

Ask:

- “What failed, and what evidence proves replacement is necessary?”
- “What repair options remain?”
- “How old is the equipment, and what parts are unavailable?”
- “Is the duct system, electrical service, insulation, or airflow causing the problem?”

Request photos of the equipment label, refrigerant readings, electrical findings, and a written diagnosis. A sales representative who jumps straight to a replacement price without documenting the failure may be selling a package, not solving your problem.

If the unit is still operating, ask for a second opinion before authorizing a same-day replacement. A temporary repair can buy time to compare bids and verify financing.

## 2. Verify the Arizona contractor and classification

Ask:

- “What is your Arizona Registrar of Contractors license number?”
- “Which classification covers the work in this contract?”
- “Will electrical, duct, or structural work be subcontracted?”
- “Who is the qualifying party for the license?”
- “Who is responsible for permits and inspections?”

Arizona requires contractors to be licensed, and ROC explains that a license classification must match the work listed in the contract. The agency also warns that homeowners sometimes hire unlicensed entities using a licensed contractor’s number.

Check the license yourself using the ROC search tool and call the number listed on the record. Do not rely only on a phone number in an online ad. Ask for current liability and workers’ compensation information as well. Arizona licensing does not automatically mean a company carries every kind of insurance you may expect.

## 3. Demand a real sizing and performance explanation

The proposal should identify:

- Heating and cooling capacity.
- SEER2 or other efficiency rating.
- Air handler or furnace match.
- Duct modifications.
- Electrical disconnect and service requirements.
- Thermostat compatibility.
- Condensate drainage and attic access.
- Start-up and commissioning steps.

Ask, “How did you size this system for my house?” A contractor who says “we always install the same tonnage” is waving a red flag. Oversized systems can cool quickly but cycle poorly, dehumidify less effectively, and create comfort problems. Undersized systems may run constantly and still leave rooms hot.

Ask who will verify airflow, refrigerant charge, static pressure, and temperature split at startup. Get the commissioning results in writing.

## 4. Read the price and financing carefully

Compare at least three written estimates using the same scope. Each quote should state what is included and excluded:

- Equipment and model numbers.
- Labor and startup.
- Crane or attic access charges.
- Duct repair.
- Electrical upgrades.
- Permit fees.
- Disposal of old equipment.
- Warranty registration.
- Maintenance plan pricing.
- Financing amount, interest rate, deferred-interest terms, and cancellation rights.

Be cautious when financing is presented as a payment rather than a total price. Ask for the cash price, financed price, total of payments, and any prepayment penalty. A low monthly payment can hide a very expensive contract.

## 5. Know who pulls the permit

Ask for the permit number once the work is scheduled and confirm who will arrange inspection. The contractor should explain whether the job includes replacing equipment only or also modifying electrical, gas, duct, roof, or structural components.

Never accept “permits are unnecessary for this replacement” without confirming with the relevant local authority. A missing permit can complicate future resale, insurance questions, warranty claims, or another contractor’s work.

## Warning signs

- The contractor refuses to provide an ROC license number.
- The license classification does not match HVAC work.
- The quote has no model numbers or efficiency ratings.
- The company will not explain system sizing.
- A full payment is required before equipment arrives.
- Financing paperwork is rushed in the driveway.
- No commissioning report is provided.
- The salesperson promises a specific energy-bill reduction.
- The proposal bundles duct, electrical, and refrigerant work without describing who performs it.

## Before you book

Verify the ROC record, compare three itemized proposals, ask how the system was sized, confirm permit responsibility, and get the startup measurements in writing. Keep the contract, model numbers, warranty registration, permit, inspection record, and commissioning sheet together.

Verify current Phoenix permit requirements and Arizona ROC rules before signing. Extreme heat is a poor time to discover that the “cheap” quote was missing half the job.
