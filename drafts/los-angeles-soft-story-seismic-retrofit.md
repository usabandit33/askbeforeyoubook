Los Angeles homeowners and small-property owners with older buildings and open ground-floor parking may face a seismic retrofit decision that is easy to misunderstand and expensive to get wrong. The city's mandatory retrofit program focuses on qualifying pre-1978 wood-frame buildings with two or more stories and an open or weak ground floor. The city also says the program generally does not apply to residential buildings with three or fewer units, so your first question is whether your property is actually in scope.

This is not a project where a salesperson should be allowed to diagnose the structure, design the fix, and pressure you into signing the same afternoon. A proper project usually involves a California-licensed engineer or architect, structural plans, a plan-check process, permits, inspections, and a contractor who can build exactly what the approved documents require.

## 1. Start with the order to comply and the building type

Ask:

- "What exact ordinance or order applies to my property?"
- "Is the building in the city's soft-story program, the non-ductile concrete program, or neither?"
- "Can you show me the building characteristics that put it in scope?"
- "Are you evaluating the whole structure, or only the parking-area wall?"

Request a copy of any city notice, prior permit history, previous structural reports, and the building's original plans if available. A contractor who says the city notice is unnecessary or that plans can be skipped is not saving you money. He is asking you to trade a visible bill for a much larger invisible risk.

## 2. Separate engineering from construction

Los Angeles requires owners to hire or contract with a California-licensed engineer or architect who prepares the technical documents. Ask for:

- The designer's California license number and discipline.
- A written scope of structural analysis.
- The proposed plans and calculations.
- A clear explanation of what is included in plan check revisions.
- The name and license information of the contractor who will build the work.

A contractor may coordinate the project, but the person responsible for structural design should be identified plainly. Be cautious when one company refuses to identify the engineer, offers only a verbal "fix," or presents a stamped plan that appears to have been copied from another building.

## 3. Compare bids on the same scope

Ask each bidder to price the same approved plan set, not a vague promise to "bring the building up to code." Your comparison should show:

- Demolition and disposal.
- New posts, beams, shear walls, hold-downs, anchors, or steel work.
- Temporary shoring.
- Concrete, welding, or drilling.
- Utility relocation and patching.
- Permit, plan-check, and inspection coordination.
- Tenant protection, dust control, and access restrictions.
- Engineering changes if concealed conditions differ.
- Cleanup, painting, and final documentation.

A low bid that excludes shoring, finish repair, or engineering revisions is not actually low. It is simply incomplete.

## 4. Verify permits, insurance, and payment terms

Ask who pulls the building permit, who schedules inspections, and who pays for re-inspections caused by defective work. Request current certificates of general liability and workers' compensation insurance, plus references for completed Los Angeles retrofit projects.

Do not accept a large cash deposit because "materials must be ordered today." Use a written contract with milestone payments tied to observable work. The contract should explain change orders, delay responsibility, warranty coverage, and what happens if the plan-check reviewer requires revisions.

## 5. Warning signs

- A salesperson says engineering is unnecessary.
- The bid is based only on photographs or a ten-minute visit.
- The contractor cannot explain which city program applies.
- There is no written scope tied to approved plans.
- The company will not provide license and insurance information.
- The contract makes you responsible for every hidden condition.
- The contractor wants full payment before inspections.
- The company promises that a permit or inspection is "just paperwork."

## Before you book

Get the city notice, verify the engineer or architect, compare at least three bids against the same plans, and confirm who will obtain permits and pass inspections. Ask for a schedule that protects tenants and parking access. Keep every plan, permit, inspection sign-off, invoice, and change order together. In Los Angeles, the paperwork is not decoration. It is the trail that proves what was designed, approved, and built.

Verify current Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety requirements before signing because ordinances, forms, and permit procedures can change.
