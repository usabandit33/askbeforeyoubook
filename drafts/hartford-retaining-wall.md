A retaining wall is not just landscaping with a little extra muscle. It holds back soil, manages water, and can affect neighboring property, walkways, driveways, foundations, and public safety. A wall that leans, bulges, cracks, or sheds soil may need more than a cosmetic patch. The real question is whether the contractor understands the load, drainage, footing, and site conditions that caused the problem.

Hartford is a useful market for this checklist because Connecticut's consumer-protection agency says home improvement is one of its most common complaint areas. In 2025, the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection said it received an average of about 1,000 home-improvement complaints annually, including poor workmanship, incomplete projects, unregistered contractors, and deposit disputes. In a 2026 warning, the agency described a complaint in which a homeowner paid nearly $9,300 for a retaining wall and the contractor performed minimal work, left the property unsafe, and did not return to finish. That is one complaint, not a market-wide failure rate. It is still a good reason to slow down before signing.

Hartford's permit guidance also matters. The city asks applicants to provide drawings, technical specifications, workers' compensation proof, a current contractor license, and a signed home-improvement contract. Hartford's permit-exemption document says a retaining wall over four feet high, measured from the bottom of the footing to the top of the wall, generally is not exempt from a building permit. Confirm the rule for your exact site, height, surcharge, and zoning before work begins.

## 1. Ask whether the wall needs repair, replacement, or engineering

- **Ask:** *"What is causing the movement, and how do you know the proposed fix addresses the cause rather than only the visible crack?"*
- **Request:** A written site assessment with photos, measurements, drainage observations, wall height, soil conditions, and a description of nearby loads such as a driveway, building, fence, or slope.

A wall may move because of hydrostatic pressure, poor drainage, inadequate footing, frost movement, erosion, surcharge from vehicles, or a failing tie-back system. A contractor who immediately proposes a new face without discussing water management may be selling a short-term cosmetic answer.

If the wall is tall, supports a driveway or structure, sits near a property line, or shows significant rotation, ask whether a licensed engineer should design or review the repair. A contractor can be competent at masonry and still not be the right person to make a structural judgment.

## 2. Verify registration, insurance, and who is actually doing the work

- **Ask:** *"What is your Connecticut Home Improvement Contractor registration number, and which legal business name will appear on the contract?"*
- **Request:** Proof of general liability insurance, workers' compensation coverage, subcontractor names, and any required professional-engineer credentials.
- **Check:** Verify the business through Connecticut's eLicense system and confirm the registration is active.

Connecticut's Department of Consumer Protection says a home-improvement contract should identify the contractor, project description, timeline, price, payment schedule, and cancellation rights. The agency also advises homeowners to verify the contractor's registration and insurance. If the work includes excavation, drainage, concrete, masonry, or engineering, ask who is responsible for each part instead of accepting a vague "turnkey" promise.

## 3. Get the permit and drainage plan in writing

- **Ask:** *"Who is pulling the building permit, what inspections are required, and how will water be collected and discharged?"*
- **Request:** Permit number, drawings, drainage details, footing dimensions, reinforcement details if applicable, and the plan for protecting adjacent property.

Hartford's building-permit page tells applicants to collect drawings, technical specifications, insurance documentation, a current contractor license, and a signed contract. The city says completed applications may take up to 30 days to process, although many are issued sooner. That means a contractor who promises to start tomorrow may be selling a schedule that does not account for the permit process.

Ask where roof runoff, wall drains, and groundwater will go. A new wall that simply traps water behind it can fail quickly. Make sure the quote states whether it includes drain rock, perforated pipe, cleanouts, outlets, grading, and restoration.

## 4. Compare complete written scopes

- **Ask:** *"Does this price include demolition, disposal, excavation, drainage, footing, wall materials, backfill, grading, permits, inspections, and cleanup?"*
- **Request:** At least two written bids using the same wall length, height, materials, and drainage assumptions.

A useful estimate should say what happens if the contractor finds buried concrete, poor soil, groundwater, roots, utility lines, or a larger footing requirement. Require written approval before extra work is performed. Avoid paying the full price before the wall and site are complete.

## Warning signs

- The contractor promises the wall will be fixed without measuring height, drainage, soil, or nearby loads.
- No permit discussion, no drawings, or no explanation of who handles inspections.
- The quote says "retaining wall" but does not identify materials, footing, drainage, or backfill.
- A contractor demands a large cash deposit or refuses traceable payment.
- The company will not provide insurance proof or subcontractor names.
- The contractor pressures you to waive the contract's cancellation period.
- The estimate leaves no written process for change orders or unsafe site conditions.

## Before you book

- Photograph and measure the wall and document cracks, leaning, drainage, and nearby structures.
- Verify the contractor's Connecticut registration and insurance.
- Confirm the permit requirement with Hartford or the authority having jurisdiction.
- Ask whether engineering review is appropriate for the wall and surrounding loads.
- Compare itemized bids that include drainage, excavation, disposal, restoration, and inspections.
- Put the payment schedule, change-order rules, and final cleanup in the contract.
- Do not treat one enforcement example as proof every contractor is dishonest, but do treat missing paperwork as a warning.

If your property is outside Hartford, local zoning, drainage, historic-district, and permit rules may differ. Verify the current requirements for your exact address with Hartford Development Services or the applicable town building office before authorizing work.
