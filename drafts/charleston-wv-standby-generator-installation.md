A standby generator can keep essential circuits running during a long outage, but a permanent installation is a substantial electrical and fuel-system project. It may involve a generator, transfer switch, electrical panel work, natural gas or propane piping, a concrete pad, permits, inspections, and ongoing service. A quote that only lists the generator model and a total price leaves too many important questions unanswered.

Charleston and Kanawha County have a strong reason to take backup power seriously. A county-level analysis of U.S. Department of Energy EAGLE-I outage records estimates that Kanawha County customers averaged 23.9 hours without power per customer in 2025. The analysis covers the county, uses a modeled customer count, and is not a measurement for each Charleston address. Separately, the U.S. Energy Information Administration reported 1,013.2 minutes of outage time per customer for West Virginia in 2025 when major-event days were included, and 498.1 minutes even when major-event days were excluded. The national figures were 269.2 and 125.3 minutes respectively. These are state-level utility averages, not a guarantee of what your home will experience, but they show that outages are a meaningful local planning concern.

Use this checklist before hiring an installer.

## 1. Which parts of the home do you need to keep running?

- **Ask:** *"Can you show me a written load calculation and list the circuits this system will support?"*

Do not rely on a salesperson's guess about generator size. The design should account for the home's electrical service, heating and cooling equipment, refrigerator, freezer, well or sump pump, medical equipment, lighting, and other essential loads. Large motors can require extra starting power, so a generator's advertised output does not tell the whole story.

Ask whether the proposal is truly designed for whole-home operation or for selected essential circuits. If it uses load management, get a list of which appliances may be delayed or disconnected when demand rises. The contract should identify the generator model, rated output, transfer equipment, and circuits included.

Be cautious if the installer recommends a size without inspecting the electrical panel or asking what you want to operate. Oversizing can increase the equipment and fuel costs without solving a real need.

## 2. Does the contractor have the required West Virginia licenses?

- **Ask:** *"What are your West Virginia contractor license number and electrical license details, and who will be responsible for each part of the work?"*

West Virginia requires a contractor license for covered construction work with a total materials-and-labor cost of $2,500 or more, subject to statutory exemptions. A typical permanent generator project can exceed that threshold. Ask for the licensed business name, license number, classification, and current status, then verify it with the West Virginia Contractor Licensing Board.

Electrical work for compensation has a separate licensing requirement. West Virginia law requires the person, firm, or corporation performing electrical work for hire to hold the appropriate license and certificate issued by the State Fire Marshal. Ask which licensed electrical contractor and electrician will perform the wiring, transfer-switch installation, and panel work. Verify the credentials through the State Fire Marshal's online license search.

Charleston also requires contractors to register with the City Collector and Building Department for local business and tax administration. Ask the city what registration applies to the company on your contract. Do not confuse city business registration with the state licenses required for contracting and electrical work.

## 3. Which permits and inspections are included?

- **Ask:** *"Who will apply for the permits, schedule every inspection, and give me the final approval records?"*

The City of Charleston says construction work within city limits requires a permit before work begins. Its Building Department issues permits for electrical work and other residential improvements, and the city inspects work as it progresses. A standby generator may involve more than one trade, depending on the electrical and fuel scope.

Ask the installer to identify each required permit, including electrical and any applicable fuel-gas, plumbing, or mechanical work. Confirm the requirements with the Charleston Building Department for the exact address. If the home is outside city limits, contact the appropriate county or other permitting authority instead.

The city specifically warns homeowners not to obtain a permit on behalf of someone else. The contractor should explain who is legally responsible for applying and should provide the permit number before work begins. Keep the inspection results and final approval with the home's records.

## 4. How will the generator connect safely to the electrical system?

- **Ask:** *"What transfer switch or approved transfer equipment is included, and how does it prevent backfeed into utility lines?"*

A permanently installed generator must be connected so the home's wiring is separated from the utility supply when generator power is in use. The transfer switch is a critical safety component. It helps prevent electricity from feeding back onto utility lines, where it can endanger line workers and damage equipment.

Get the transfer-switch model, circuit list, and installation scope in writing. Ask the electrician to explain how the system changes from utility power to generator power and back again. Ask whether the main panel needs replacement, additional breakers, or load-management equipment.

Never accept a proposal that uses a male-to-male cord, plugs the generator into a wall outlet, or bypasses the transfer equipment. The CPSC identifies backfeeding as an electrocution hazard to utility workers and neighbors.

## 5. Where will the generator sit, and how will it be fueled?

- **Ask:** *"Can you show the proposed location and explain the manufacturer's clearances, exhaust direction, noise, drainage, and fuel-line requirements?"*

The location affects maintenance access, noise, exhaust safety, property lines, drainage, and the length and cost of the electrical and fuel connections. The installer should follow the exact generator manufacturer's instructions and the applicable building, electrical, fuel-gas, and fire requirements.

If the unit uses natural gas, ask whether the existing meter, regulator, and gas line can supply the required flow while other gas appliances are operating. If it uses propane, ask whether the tank and regulator are sized for the generator and how fuel delivery will work during an extended outage. Identify who is responsible for the fuel piping, pressure testing where required, permits, and inspections.

Do not apply portable-generator placement rules blindly to a permanently installed standby unit. The fixed unit must follow its listing, manufacturer's clearances, and applicable local code. Still, exhaust must not be directed toward doors, windows, vents, or places where people may be exposed to carbon monoxide. Ask where carbon monoxide alarms should be located in the home.

## 6. What exactly is included in the quote?

- **Ask:** *"Can you itemize equipment, electrical work, fuel work, site preparation, permits, inspections, startup, and optional maintenance?"*

Compare proposals using the same generator capacity and the same circuit list. Check whether each quote includes the generator, transfer switch, pad, wiring, conduit, trenching, fuel-line upgrades, gas regulator, propane equipment if needed, permit fees, delivery, startup, and cleanup.

Ask for the exact model number and the manufacturer's warranty. Get the installer's workmanship warranty separately. Find out who registers the equipment, what maintenance is required to preserve the warranty, and who will service the generator if the original installer is unavailable.

A low equipment price can become an expensive project if the quote excludes panel work, fuel piping, permits, or a required pad. Ask the installer to list exclusions and explain how extra work will be priced and approved.

## 7. What happens if the job is delayed or the contractor does not finish?

- **Ask:** *"What is the deposit for, what are the payment milestones, and what happens if the equipment or installation date changes?"*

Use a written contract that identifies the licensed business, model and capacity, scope, price, start and completion estimates, permit responsibilities, warranty, cancellation terms, and payment schedule. Tie payments to clear milestones such as equipment delivery, approved installation work, and successful commissioning. Avoid paying the full amount before the equipment is delivered and the work is completed.

In March 2026, the West Virginia Attorney General filed a lawsuit against a Putnam County contractor after receiving several complaints alleging that consumers paid for home-improvement work that was not completed or was left unfinished without refunds. The case concerned a roofing and framing business, not generator installers, and the allegations are not a finding that every contractor is dishonest. It is a useful reminder to verify the business, get the agreement in writing, and avoid large advance payments without safeguards.

Be wary of pressure to sign immediately, a price that is dramatically lower than competing bids, vague equipment descriptions, requests for cash without a receipt, or refusal to provide license and insurance information.

## 8. Will the system be tested before the installer leaves?

- **Ask:** *"Will you demonstrate an automatic outage transfer, run the generator under load, and give me the commissioning and warranty records?"*

A generator that starts in a demonstration is not necessarily ready to power the home. Ask the installer to test the automatic transfer, verify the selected circuits, check fuel supply, and demonstrate the return to utility power. Ask for written startup or commissioning records and any required inspection approval.

Have the installer show you the exercise schedule, shutdown procedure, alarm indicators, service intervals, and how to contact support. Ask what happens if the generator fails a self-test or will not start during an outage. A maintenance agreement should identify what is included, what costs extra, and how quickly emergency service is available.

## Before you book

- Compare at least three detailed proposals when practical.
- Verify the state contractor license and the State Fire Marshal electrical credentials.
- Confirm the company's local Charleston business registration and insurance.
- Get the electrical, fuel, and other permit requirements in writing for your address.
- Require a load calculation, circuit list, generator model, transfer-switch details, and fuel-system scope.
- Check placement, exhaust direction, drainage, noise, and manufacturer clearances.
- Get the deposit, milestones, completion terms, warranty, and maintenance plan in the contract.
- Keep permit numbers, inspection results, startup records, and warranty documents.

Charleston's outage history makes backup power a reasonable project to investigate, but a generator is a safety system, not just a large appliance. Choose an installer who can explain the design, licensing, permits, and commissioning without pressure. Verify current requirements with the City of Charleston, West Virginia Contractor Licensing Board, and State Fire Marshal before signing.
