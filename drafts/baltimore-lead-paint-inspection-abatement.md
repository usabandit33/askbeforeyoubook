Baltimore's older housing stock makes lead paint a practical issue for homeowners, buyers, landlords, and families planning renovations. The age of a house does not prove that lead paint is present, but it should prompt careful questions before disturbing painted surfaces. Dust from deteriorated paint or renovation work can create a serious exposure hazard, especially for young children.

The Maryland State Data Center's 2020–2024 American Community Survey 5-year estimates report 295,032 housing units in Baltimore City. About 81.6% were built before 1980, calculated by adding the reported 1970–1979, 1960–1969, 1950–1959, 1940–1949, and 1939-or-earlier categories. This is a housing-age indicator, not a count of homes with lead paint. Federal lead-based-paint rules generally focus on housing built before 1978, so a substantial share of Baltimore's homes fall within the age range where lead-safe precautions deserve attention.

There is also a concrete reason to verify credentials and paperwork rather than accepting a certificate at face value. On July 2, 2026, Maryland's Attorney General announced an $8.7 million judgment against a Baltimore lead-paint inspector and a permanent industry ban following a criminal conviction for falsifying lead paint certificates. The case led to the revocation of more than 1,400 inspection certificates statewide. This enforcement case does not mean every local inspector is untrustworthy. It does show why homeowners should independently confirm accreditation and keep the underlying inspection records.

## 1. Be clear about the service you are hiring

A lead inspection, risk assessment, lead hazard reduction, and full abatement are not interchangeable services. An inspection may identify whether lead-based paint is present. A risk assessment evaluates lead hazards and may recommend ways to reduce exposure. Abatement is a defined process intended to permanently eliminate a lead-based-paint hazard, while Maryland rental compliance can involve specific risk-reduction standards and dust testing.

- **Ask:** *"Are you performing a lead paint inspection, a risk assessment, a rental risk-reduction inspection, or abatement?"*
- **Ask:** *"What decision will this report or certificate allow me to make?"*
- **Ask:** *"Does the scope cover interior surfaces, exterior surfaces, windows, doors, friction surfaces, and common areas where applicable?"*
- **Request:** A written description of the service, the inspection method, the areas included, and the exact document you will receive.

Do not hire a company based only on a promise to provide a "lead-safe certificate." Ask what standard is being evaluated, which agency recognizes the document, and whether the service is appropriate for your goal. If you are buying a home, renovating your own home, or complying with rental rules, explain that before the contractor quotes the job.

## 2. Verify Maryland accreditation before paying

Maryland requires accreditation for lead paint inspection and abatement work covered by state law. The Maryland Department of the Environment (MDE) maintains information about accredited inspectors, risk assessors, contractors, and supervisors. Verify both the company and the individual who will perform or supervise the work.

- **Ask:** *"What is your current MDE accreditation type and number?"*
- **Ask:** *"Which accredited individual will actually inspect or supervise this project?"*
- **Ask:** *"Is your company accredited for the specific service in this proposal?"*
- **Check:** Use MDE's lead certification and accreditation resources or contact the department directly if the online record is unclear.
- **Request:** Current accreditation details, the written scope, and proof of insurance appropriate to the work.

A contractor may be accredited for one role but not another. Do not assume that a general home-improvement license, EPA training card, or a different employee's credential authorizes the person standing in your home to perform every lead-related task. Ask MDE to clarify the credential that applies to your specific service.

## 3. If this is a rental property, understand Maryland's registration and inspection rules

Maryland's lead-risk-reduction law applies to covered pre-1978 rental dwelling units statewide. Maryland OneStop explains that owners must register covered rental units, including all units, within 30 days after acquiring the property, renew registration every two years, and pay the applicable registration fee. Inspections are required at specified triggering events and must be conducted by an MDE-accredited lead paint inspector.

The exact inspection path depends on the property's status and circumstances. MDE describes options including Lead Free certification, Full Risk Reduction certification, and Modified Risk Reduction certification. A Full Risk Reduction inspection generally includes dust sampling in a vacant unit before a new tenant moves in. Modified Risk Reduction may apply in response to defective paint conditions or certain notices involving a child's or pregnant woman's blood lead level.

- **Ask:** *"Which Maryland rental inspection or certification option applies to this unit, and why?"*
- **Ask:** *"Is the unit registered with MDE, and is the registration current?"*
- **Ask:** *"Will the inspection include dust samples and laboratory analysis if the selected standard requires them?"*
- **Ask:** *"Who submits the certificate and supporting documents to MDE, and when will I receive copies?"*
- **Request:** The rental registration record, inspection report, laboratory results where applicable, certificate, and any notices or correction records.

Do not assume that a certificate for one unit covers every unit in a multi-unit property. Ask the inspector to identify the exact address and unit number on the paperwork.

## 4. For renovation, ask how the contractor will control lead dust

Federal EPA Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) requirements generally apply to paid renovation, repair, and painting work that disturbs painted surfaces in pre-1978 housing and child-occupied facilities, subject to the rule's scope and exceptions. Maryland accreditation requirements may also apply. A contractor should explain how the work will be contained and cleaned, not merely promise to be careful.

- **Ask:** *"Does this project require an EPA-certified renovation firm and a certified renovator?"*
- **Ask:** *"How will you isolate the work area, protect belongings, control dust, and clean before reopening the space?"*
- **Ask:** *"Will you provide the required pre-renovation information and records?"*
- **Ask:** *"If testing identifies a hazard, who is qualified to design and perform the next step?"*
- **Request:** Firm and individual credentials, the written work plan, containment and cleanup procedures, and copies of required project records.

Be wary of dry sanding, open-flame paint removal, uncontrolled demolition, or a contractor who dismisses lead concerns because the paint looks intact. Do not disturb suspect paint yourself just to see what is underneath.

## 5. Get complete reports and certificates, not just a one-page promise

The report should identify the property, date, inspector, accreditation, areas examined, methods used, findings, limitations, and recommended next steps. If samples were collected, ask for the laboratory name and the results. If work was performed, request the work records and the appropriate clearance or risk-reduction documentation.

- **Ask:** *"Will the report list every area inspected and any areas that were inaccessible or excluded?"*
- **Ask:** *"If a sample was taken, can I see the lab result and sample location?"*
- **Ask:** *"What does this certificate establish, and what does it not establish?"*
- **Ask:** *"Who can I contact at MDE to verify this certificate or accreditation?"*
- **Request:** Full report, sample results, work records, photographs, invoices, clearance or risk-reduction documents, and copies of anything submitted to MDE.

Keep records permanently with the property file, especially if you rent, refinance, renovate, or sell the home. A lead-related certificate is not a guarantee that no lead exists anywhere in the building; it applies to the scope and standard described in the underlying documentation.

## Warning signs before you book

- The contractor will not provide an MDE accreditation number or the name of the person doing the work.
- The company promises a certificate before inspecting the property.
- The proposal does not identify the rooms, surfaces, units, or standards covered.
- The contractor suggests disturbing old paint without a lead-safe work plan.
- You receive a certificate with a mismatched address, unit, date, inspector, or accreditation.
- The company refuses to provide the full report, lab results, or supporting documentation.
- A landlord or contractor tells you that registration or inspection is unnecessary without explaining the property's status and the applicable exception.

## Before you book

- Identify whether you need an inspection, risk assessment, rental risk-reduction inspection, renovation service, or abatement.
- Verify the company and individual credentials with Maryland MDE.
- Confirm whether the property is covered by Maryland's pre-1978 rental requirements.
- Ask for a written scope and the exact report or certificate you will receive.
- Confirm EPA RRP requirements for covered renovation work.
- Keep reports, lab results, work records, and certificates with the property file.
- Contact MDE or the EPA if a credential, certificate, or legal requirement is unclear.

Baltimore City is the local focus, but Maryland's lead rental law and accreditation system are statewide. Local housing or rental requirements may add obligations. Before hiring, confirm current rules with MDE, Baltimore City housing officials, and EPA as applicable to your property and project. If a certificate looks questionable, contact MDE directly instead of relying only on the contractor who issued it.
