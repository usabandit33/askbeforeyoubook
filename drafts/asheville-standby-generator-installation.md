A standby generator can make a difficult outage easier to manage, but the installation is not just a matter of setting a machine beside the house. The project may involve electrical work, a transfer switch, natural gas or propane piping, a concrete pad, permits, inspections, and coordination with the utility.

Asheville has a real reason to plan carefully. During Hurricane Helene in September 2024, roughly 100,000 customers in Buncombe County lost electricity, and the storm also disrupted Asheville's water system. The U.S. Energy Information Administration reported 1,518.1 minutes of interruption per customer for North Carolina in 2024 when major-event days were included, compared with 662.6 minutes nationally. Those are statewide annual figures, not a prediction for one Asheville home, and the extraordinary storm year heavily influenced them. They do show why backup-power decisions deserve more than a rushed sales pitch.

Use this checklist to compare installers before signing a contract.

## 1. What do you actually want the generator to power?

- **Ask:** *"Can you show me the load calculation and explain which circuits this generator will support?"*

A generator advertised as "whole house" may not run every large appliance at the same time. The right size depends on the home's electrical service, starting loads, heating and cooling equipment, well or sump pumps, cooking appliances, and the circuits you want to prioritize.

Ask the estimator to identify the loads included in the proposal. If the design relies on load management, ask which appliances may be delayed or disconnected when demand is high. Get the proposed generator model, rated output, and transfer equipment in writing.

Be cautious if the salesperson chooses a size without reviewing the electrical panel or asking what you want to run. A bigger machine is not automatically a better design. It can mean higher equipment, fuel, and maintenance costs.

## 2. Who is responsible for the electrical work?

- **Ask:** *"What is the North Carolina electrical contractor license number for the business doing the wiring, and who is the qualified individual supervising the job?"*

North Carolina law requires an applicable state electrical-contractor license to engage in electrical contracting. Asheville's code also says electrical permits are issued to people holding a valid license from the North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors.

Check the contractor in the Board's active license search. Confirm that the license is current and that its classification covers the work being proposed. If the generator dealer is using a separate electrician, identify that company in the contract rather than accepting "our installer is licensed" as the whole answer.

A generator sales certification or manufacturer-dealer status is not a substitute for the electrical license required for the electrical work.

## 3. Which permits and inspections are included?

- **Ask:** *"Who will apply for each permit, schedule the inspections, and give me the final approval records?"*

The City of Asheville lists generators that are primarily electrical with gas work among the projects that may use a multi-trade permit. The application needs to identify all trades involved. The exact permit path depends on the scope and the property's permitting jurisdiction.

If the home is outside Asheville city limits, check with Buncombe County rather than assuming the city will handle it. Ask the installer to list the electrical and fuel-gas permits that apply, who will pull them, and what inspections are required.

Do not accept a promise to "take care of permits later." A permit is part of the job, not an optional upgrade. Keep copies of the permit applications, inspection results, and final approvals with the home's records.

## 4. How will the generator connect safely to the house?

- **Ask:** *"What transfer switch or approved transfer equipment is included, and how does the design prevent power from feeding back onto utility lines?"*

A permanent generator needs an approved way to separate the home's circuits from the utility supply when generator power is in use. A properly selected and installed transfer switch helps prevent dangerous backfeed that can injure utility workers, damage equipment, or create a fire hazard.

The proposal should identify the transfer switch model, the circuits it controls, and whether the work requires changes to the main panel. Ask the electrician to explain the switching sequence in plain language.

Walk away from anyone who suggests connecting a generator directly to a wall outlet or bypassing the transfer equipment. Do not allow a contractor to energize the system before the required inspection and testing.

## 5. Where will the unit sit, and how will it be fueled?

- **Ask:** *"Can you show the proposed location on a site plan and explain the required clearances, exhaust direction, flood exposure, and fuel-line work?"*

The location affects noise, access for maintenance, exhaust safety, property-line concerns, drainage, and the length and cost of electrical and fuel connections. The installer should follow the exact generator manufacturer's installation instructions and applicable fire, building, electrical, and fuel-gas requirements.

If the unit will use natural gas or propane, ask who is responsible for sizing and installing the fuel line, checking available supply, pressure-testing the piping where required, and obtaining any related permit. Ask whether a propane tank, regulator, or meter upgrade is needed and whether those costs are included.

A quote that says only "generator installed" leaves too much room for expensive surprises.

## 6. What is included in the price?

- **Ask:** *"Can I have an itemized, fixed-scope proposal that separates equipment, electrical work, fuel work, site preparation, permits, inspections, startup, and optional maintenance?"*

Compare proposals using the same generator capacity and the same list of circuits. Check whether each includes the pad, trenching, conduit, wiring, transfer switch, gas or propane connections, utility coordination, delivery, taxes, and cleanup.

Ask for the manufacturer's warranty and the installer's separate workmanship warranty. Find out who registers the generator, what maintenance is required to preserve the warranty, and whether the installer or another company will service it locally.

Do not compare only the equipment price. A low quote may omit fuel-line upgrades, panel changes, permit fees, or commissioning.

## 7. What happens if delivery or installation is delayed?

- **Ask:** *"What is the deposit used for, what are the payment milestones, and what happens if the equipment or installation date changes?"*

Get the model number, estimated delivery date, scope, cancellation terms, refund terms, and completion deadline in the written agreement. Avoid paying the full contract amount before the equipment is delivered and the work is completed.

North Carolina's Attorney General warned homeowners rebuilding after Helene about contractor fraud and recommends checking businesses, getting multiple estimates, and putting the work in writing. A contractor who pressures you to sign immediately, demands an unusually large cash deposit, or refuses to identify the actual installer deserves extra scrutiny.

## Before you book

- Verify the electrical contractor's active North Carolina license and the correct classification.
- Confirm whether the property is in Asheville's permitting jurisdiction or Buncombe County's.
- Get a written load calculation, equipment model, transfer-switch details, and circuit list.
- Get the fuel-line scope, generator location, permit plan, inspection plan, and all exclusions in writing.
- Ask for insurance information, warranty terms, service arrangements, and realistic delivery milestones.
- Keep the permit, inspection, startup, and warranty documents after the work is complete.

A generator is a safety system, not just a large appliance. Compare at least three detailed proposals when practical, and do not let storm memories or a salesperson's countdown clock make the decision for you. Verify current permit, electrical, gas, and fire requirements with the City of Asheville or Buncombe County for your exact address before work begins.
