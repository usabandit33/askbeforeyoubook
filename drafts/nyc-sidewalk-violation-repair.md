A New York City sidewalk violation is not just a note about cracked concrete. It can become a recorded property issue that complicates a sale or refinance, and the city gives owners a short window to act. Property owners may also be liable for personal injuries caused by a failure to maintain the adjoining sidewalk free of defects. The contractor you hire also has to understand NYC Department of Transportation specifications, sidewalk permits, and the final dismissal inspection. A low quote that does not clear the violation is not a bargain.

NYC DOT says a property owner generally has 75 days after receiving a Notice of Sidewalk Violation to make the required repairs. If the owner does not act within that period, DOT may arrange the work and bill the owner. The city says there is no fine attached to the violation itself, but the notice remains on file with the County Clerk until the city confirms satisfactory repairs. Read the notice and its Preliminary Inspection Report before asking contractors to bid.

## 1. Does the notice identify exactly what must be replaced?

Start with the Preliminary Inspection Report (PIR) that came with the notice. It includes a sketch and numbered sidewalk flags. NYC DOT says every flag numbered on the report must be replaced and the defects corrected. A contractor who proposes to patch only the visible crack, grind one raised edge, or replace fewer flags may leave you with an unresolved violation.

**Ask:** "Will your written estimate list every numbered flag on my PIR and show exactly what you will replace?"

Ask the contractor to walk the frontage with you and compare the report to the actual sidewalk. Check the location of trees, utility covers, cellar doors, signs, curb ramps, and other features. If the report appears to identify the wrong property or the wrong sidewalk squares, contact NYC311 about a reinspection before signing a repair contract. DOT says a reinspection may be requested within the same 75-day period. Do not assume a contractor can correct an inaccurate violation by doing extra concrete work.

## 2. Is the contractor licensed for sidewalk work in New York City?

NYC's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP) issues the Home Improvement Contractor license. The city's business licensing page specifically includes sidewalks among the types of residential land improvements covered by the license. DCWP says a license is required for covered work costing more than $200 at a residential building or on residential land.

**Ask:** "What is the exact legal business name and DCWP Home Improvement Contractor license number that will appear on my contract?"

Check the name and status yourself using DCWP's current license lookup. A company may advertise under a trade name that differs from its legal license holder. Make sure the company signing your contract is the company whose license you verified. Ask who will actually perform the work and whether any subcontractor will be involved.

DCWP's June 2026 consumer tips explain that licensed contractors may provide access to the Home Improvement Contractor Trust Fund if a consumer is owed money and DCWP cannot resolve the matter. The tips also explain that an unlicensed contractor does not provide the same trust-fund protection. The city publishes a complaint-history lookup, so check complaints rather than relying only on online star ratings.

## 3. Who obtains the sidewalk construction permit?

NYC DOT requires a sidewalk construction permit before repair work begins. The current DOT guidance lists a permit fee of $70 for each 300 linear feet of sidewalk for one property. A corner lot or through lot may need more than one permit. The permit does not extend the 75-day repair deadline.

If you hire a contractor, DOT says the contractor must apply for the permit through the city's street-permit system. Ask for the permit application number and a copy of the issued permit before work starts. Do not accept "we do this all the time" as proof that the permit is handled.

**Ask:** "Is the permit included in your price, who is responsible for obtaining it, and when will I receive the permit number?"

Compare the permit scope with the work shown on your PIR. If the contractor says no permit is needed, ask DOT to confirm that claim for your exact work. Root removal, unclogging, or other minor work can be treated differently from sidewalk reconstruction, so do not generalize an exception to a full repair.

## 4. Are the bids comparing the same work and finish?

Get at least two or three written estimates from licensed contractors. Ask each one to price the same numbered flags and to identify the work that will be done to meet DOT requirements. A useful estimate should spell out:

- Demolition and removal of the existing concrete.
- Number and dimensions of sidewalk flags to be replaced.
- Base preparation, forming, concrete placement, finish, and curing.
- Hauling and disposal of debris.
- Protection of trees, utility covers, cellar doors, and adjacent property.
- Pedestrian barriers, signs, and worksite safety.
- Permit fees and any required street or occupancy permits.
- Who will arrange the DOT dismissal inspection and respond if the work is rejected.

Do not compare a full replacement quote with a patching quote as if they were the same service. If the contractor recommends an alternative to full flag replacement, ask the contractor to identify the DOT rule or written approval that makes the alternative acceptable for your violation.

## 5. What contract, insurance, and payment protections do I get?

DCWP's consumer guidance says home improvement contractors must provide a written contract signed by both sides. The contract should describe the work and materials, schedule, payment terms, charges, guarantees, and warranties. It must include a separate Notice of Cancellation form. DCWP also says not to pay more than 25% of the total contract amount up front to start the work.

**Ask:** "Can I have the full contract, cancellation notice, insurance certificates, and permit details before I pay a deposit?"

Request proof of general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage, and confirm that the policy covers the actual contractor and worksite. Ask whether the quote includes any work required to protect or restore the area around the sidewalk. Keep copies of the notice, PIR, estimates, contract, permit, photos, invoices, and payment receipts.

## 6. How will the violation be cleared after construction?

Finishing the concrete is not the last step. After the work is complete, the owner should request a DOT sidewalk violation dismissal inspection. DOT needs the repair to be visible so the inspector can check it against the violation. Ask the contractor to explain who will request the inspection, what information is needed, and how any failed inspection or correction will be handled.

**Ask:** "Will you provide the permit number, completion photos, inspection request details, and written confirmation when DOT clears the violation?"

Do not release the final payment simply because the crew has left. Review the finished work, confirm the permit is closed as required, and check the violation status through NYC311 or the relevant city record. Keep the final clearance with your property records.

## Warning signs

- The contractor refuses to provide a current DCWP license number.
- The estimate does not match the numbered flags on the PIR.
- The contractor promises that a patch will clear the violation without showing the DOT basis.
- The contractor says a permit is unnecessary for a full sidewalk reconstruction.
- The quote excludes disposal, barriers, or permit work without explaining why.
- The contractor demands cash, a blank contract, or more than 25% up front.
- The company will not identify who is doing the work or who handles the dismissal inspection.
- The contractor pressures you to sign immediately because the 75-day period is running.

## Before you book

Read the violation notice and PIR first. Verify the contractor's DCWP license under the exact legal name, compare written bids against every numbered flag, confirm the permit plan, and put the dismissal inspection in the contract. If the notice appears incorrect, ask NYC DOT about a reinspection within the deadline instead of paying for work that may not be necessary. Verify current permit fees, specifications, licensing status, and local requirements directly with NYC DOT and DCWP before work begins.
