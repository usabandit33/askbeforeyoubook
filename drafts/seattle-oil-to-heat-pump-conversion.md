Seattle homeowners replacing oil heat with a heat pump are not just buying a box on the side of the house. The project can include load calculations, ductwork or refrigerant lines, electrical capacity, thermostat controls, oil-tank decommissioning, permits, and rebate paperwork. A low equipment price can become an expensive project if those pieces are left vague.

Seattle's Clean Heat program currently advertises a $2,000 instant rebate for qualifying conversions and a possible additional income-qualified bonus. The city also says the oil tank must be decommissioned under Seattle Fire Department requirements. Rebates are helpful, but they also create a temptation for contractors to sell the incentive before they explain the actual scope.

## 1. Confirm the existing system and the home's heating load

Ask:

- "What is the measured heating and cooling load for my home?"
- "Will the heat pump serve every room, or will another heat source remain?"
- "Is the current ductwork sized and sealed for the proposed equipment?"
- "What happens on the coldest design days?"

Request the model numbers, capacity, efficiency ratings, and a written explanation of whether the system is ducted, ductless, or a hybrid. Ask whether the installer performed a real load calculation or simply matched the new unit to the old oil furnace. Oversizing can hurt comfort and cycling, while undersizing can leave rooms cold.

## 2. Separate the heat-pump work from the oil-tank work

Seattle's program materials specifically connect the conversion with tank decommissioning. Ask who will inspect, pump out, remove, abandon, or otherwise decommission the tank, and under which rules. Request a separate line item for tank work, disposal, permits, soil testing if needed, and any restoration.

Do not assume the HVAC company is qualified to perform environmental or tank work. Ask for the name and credentials of the tank contractor, proof of insurance, and a written plan for what happens if a leak is discovered. A tank leak can turn a clean heating upgrade into a cleanup project.

## 3. Verify the installer and rebate paperwork

Ask:

- "Are you a participating contractor for the specific Seattle program?"
- "What equipment qualifies for the rebate?"
- "Who completes the homeowner participation form?"
- "When is the rebate applied, and what happens if the application is rejected?"
- "Which permits and inspections are included?"

Seattle recommends at least three bids and says contractor discounts may depend on participating distributors. Request the exact rebate terms in writing, not just a marketing flyer. The contract should state the gross price, rebate, any utility discount, and the amount you owe before work begins.

## 4. Review electrical, condensate, and code details

A heat-pump conversion may require a new circuit, disconnect, panel work, condensate management, line-set routing, weatherproofing, or structural support. Ask whether the home's electrical service has enough capacity and whether a panel upgrade is included or excluded.

Request a permit plan, equipment placement drawing, and explanation of how condensate will drain in cold weather. Ask who repairs wall penetrations, drywall, siding, decks, or landscaping after installation. These are not small details when the project crosses several trades.

## 5. Compare comfort, maintenance, and warranty

Ask whether the price includes startup commissioning, airflow balancing, thermostat setup, homeowner training, and a first-year service visit. Clarify labor warranty versus manufacturer warranty. Ask who performs warranty service if the installing company closes or stops serving the area.

A contractor who promises lower bills without discussing insulation, air leakage, electricity rates, and maintenance is overselling. Heat pumps can be excellent, but they still obey physics, and physics does not accept promotional coupons as payment.

## Warning signs

- The contractor cannot produce a load calculation.
- The rebate is promised before the equipment is identified.
- Tank decommissioning is mentioned only verbally.
- The bid excludes permits, electrical changes, or finish repair.
- No one explains cold-weather operation or backup heat.
- The company will not provide license, insurance, or references.
- A large deposit is demanded before the scope is complete.

## Before you book

Get three bids with the same equipment and scope, verify the installer and rebate requirements, separate tank work from HVAC work, and confirm permit responsibility. Ask for written model numbers, efficiency ratings, warranty terms, startup commissioning, and the tank decommissioning plan. Verify current Seattle, Washington, and utility requirements before signing because rebates and program deadlines can change.
