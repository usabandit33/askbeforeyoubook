Radon is invisible, odorless, and not something a contractor can diagnose from a quick glance at your basement. In Denver County, the EPA classifies the area as Zone 1, the highest potential category for elevated indoor radon. That does not tell you what is in your house, but it does make testing a sensible first step. Colorado also regulates radon measurement and mitigation professionals, which gives homeowners more to verify than a contractor's logo and a confident handshake.

The goal is not to buy the biggest fan or the scariest sales pitch. It is to understand the test, choose the right system for the building, and make sure the finished work is documented and retested.

## 1. Start with a defensible test

- **Ask:** Who placed the test, where was it located, and how long did it run?
- **Request:** The laboratory report, test device information, dates, room location, and any notes about closed-house conditions.
- **Watch for:** A contractor who skips testing, treats one old result as permanent proof, or says a free visual inspection can replace a measurement.

The EPA says all homes should be tested, including homes outside the highest zone. A test result is not a diagnosis of the contractor. It is a measurement of one home under specific conditions. If you are buying or selling, ask whether the test was performed under the applicable real-estate testing protocol and whether the result is recent enough for the transaction.

## 2. Verify Colorado credentials before discussing price

Colorado created a licensing framework for radon measurement and mitigation professionals. Ask for the individual's or company's current Colorado license information and confirm it through the state licensing system before signing.

- **Ask:** Who is the licensed radon measurement or mitigation professional responsible for this project?
- **Request:** License number, business name, proof of general liability insurance, workers' compensation coverage if employees are used, and references for similar homes.
- **Ask again:** Will the same licensed professional design, supervise, and document the installation, or will the work be handed to another crew?

A low quote from an unlicensed or vague provider is not a bargain if the system fails, the fan becomes a noise problem, or you need to pay a second contractor to correct the first installation.

## 3. Compare the system design, not just the total

A useful proposal should explain why the contractor selected sub-slab suction, crawl-space work, sump-pit suction, or another approach. It should identify the suction point, piping route, fan location, electrical needs, condensate or water issues, sealing work, and how the system will be monitored.

- **Ask:** What building features could interfere with performance?
- **Request:** A simple diagram, equipment model numbers, warranty terms, expected operating noise, and the plan for sealing major openings.
- **Ask:** What happens if the first post-install test is still elevated?

Be cautious when a proposal promises a guaranteed number without explaining how the home will be tested afterward. A responsible contractor can describe a target and a verification plan, but no honest person can guarantee that every house will behave identically.

## 4. Get the local permit and inspection answer in writing

Permit and inspection rules can vary by city, county, and the type of electrical work involved. Ask the contractor to identify the exact authority having jurisdiction for your address and state who is responsible for permits, inspections, and correction notices.

- **Request:** Permit numbers when required, the inspection process, and a copy of any final approval.
- **Ask:** Is a new electrical circuit, exterior penetration, roof or wall flashing, or condensate discharge involved?
- **Warning sign:** “We never need permits” without checking the address.

Colorado law gives the state authority to inspect radon measurement locations and mitigation installations in response to complaints or other information. Documentation matters because it lets you prove what was installed and who was responsible if there is a dispute later.

## Warning signs

- The seller or contractor pressures you to sign before you receive the laboratory report.
- The quote is a one-line number with no system diagram or scope.
- The contractor says licensing is unnecessary because the work is “just a fan.”
- The proposal omits retesting, warranty limits, electrical work, or who repairs a failed system.
- The contractor wants full payment before installation, documentation, and the agreed post-install test.
- The contractor refuses to identify the person legally responsible for the work.

## Before you book

- [ ] Save the original test report and confirm the result is tied to your address.
- [ ] Verify the Colorado radon professional license.
- [ ] Get at least two written proposals with system diagrams.
- [ ] Compare fan warranty, labor warranty, noise expectations, and retesting terms.
- [ ] Confirm permit and inspection responsibilities for your exact Denver address.
- [ ] Pay by milestones tied to installation and documentation, not promises.
- [ ] Schedule the post-install test exactly as the proposal requires.

A sound contractor will make the invisible problem more measurable, not more mysterious. Ask for records, compare like with like, and do not let urgency replace evidence.
