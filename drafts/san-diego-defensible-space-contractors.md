San Diego homeowners in Very High Fire Severity Zones may be responsible for maintaining defensible space around habitable structures. The work can include clearing dead vegetation, pruning trees, removing combustible material, improving the first five feet around the home, and coordinating larger home-hardening projects. It is also easy to hire the wrong kind of contractor for the job.

A brush-clearing crew is not automatically qualified to repair a roof, install ember-resistant vents, move electrical equipment, or perform structural work. Start by defining the risk-reduction task, then hire each trade for the work it is licensed and insured to perform.

## 1. Find out what your property actually requires

Ask:

- "Is my home inside the city's Very High Fire Severity Zone or another local wildland-urban interface area?"
- "Which rules apply to my address: city, county, fire district, HOA, or all of them?"
- "Do I need a defensible-space inspection or a written risk assessment?"
- "Which work is required, and which work is merely recommended?"

San Diego's guidance divides brush management into zones extending up to 100 feet, while newer Zone 0 rules focus on the first five feet around the structure. The County of San Diego also notes that fire-district requirements can differ. Do not assume a contractor's generic "100-foot clearance" package matches your actual address.

## 2. Separate property cleanup from home hardening

Create two lists before requesting bids.

**Defensible-space work may include:**

- Removing dead plants, leaves, needles, debris, and firewood.
- Pruning branches away from roofs and walls.
- Clearing or thinning vegetation by zone.
- Hauling and disposal.
- Follow-up maintenance.

**Home-hardening work may include:**

- Roof or gutter improvements.
- Ember-resistant vents.
- Exterior doors and windows.
- Deck or fence changes.
- Repairs near eaves, soffits, and attached structures.

Ask which license classification covers each item. A company may be excellent at vegetation management but not qualified to perform roofing, electrical, masonry, or structural work. A single invoice should not hide multiple trades.

## 3. Verify licensing, insurance, and disposal

Request:

- The contractor's California license number and classification, when required.
- General liability and workers' compensation certificates.
- Proof that tree work is covered by the policy.
- A written disposal plan and destination for green waste.
- References for properties with similar terrain and access.

Ask whether the crew will use climbing, chipping, chainsaws, or heavy equipment. Confirm who is responsible for damage to fences, irrigation, retaining walls, driveways, septic systems, or neighboring property. If the work is near power lines, ask who coordinates with the utility and whether a qualified line-clearance arborist is needed.

## 4. Do not let insurance urgency replace inspection

Insurance notices can create legitimate deadlines, but they also create a perfect setting for rushed, overpriced work. Ask the contractor to separate:

- Work required by a written insurer notice.
- Work required by local law or fire-district rules.
- Work recommended as a risk reduction.
- Optional landscaping or cosmetic work.

Request before-and-after photographs, measurements by zone, an itemized invoice, and a maintenance schedule. If a contractor promises that one treatment will guarantee insurance approval or wildfire survival, the promise is too strong. No honest contractor can guarantee a home will survive every fire.

San Diego County has offered assistance programs for some qualifying homeowners, but program availability and application status can change. Ask the county or fire district directly rather than paying a contractor to "unlock" a grant.

## 5. Compare the bid, not the slogan

A useful quote should identify the property, work zones, vegetation or structures included, access limitations, hauling, disposal, permits, inspections, follow-up visits, and exclusions. Ask whether the price is one-time or recurring.

For home-hardening work, compare product specifications and installation details, not just brand names. Ask who will pull permits, who schedules inspections, and what warranty covers labor versus materials.

## Warning signs

- "Insurance approved" is promised without reviewing the written notice.
- The company cannot identify the applicable zone or local rule.
- Brush clearing and roof, vent, or electrical work are bundled without separate scopes.
- No proof of liability or workers' compensation insurance.
- A large deposit is demanded before an address-specific plan.
- The quote has no disposal, restoration, or maintenance terms.
- The contractor says permits are never needed.
- The company refuses to provide photographs or a written completion report.

## Before you book

Confirm the rules for your address, define the work by zone and trade, verify licenses and insurance, and get at least three itemized bids. Ask for a written completion report with photographs that you can keep for your insurer, HOA, or future buyer. Verify current City of San Diego, County of San Diego, and fire-district requirements before signing because wildfire standards and program details can change.
