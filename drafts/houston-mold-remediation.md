Houston homeowners know that water can arrive through more than a dramatic hurricane. A roof leak, plumbing failure, air-conditioning condensation problem, or neighborhood flooding can leave moisture in wall cavities, flooring, insulation, and cabinets. Mold cleanup is not simply a matter of spraying a visible patch. The source of moisture has to be identified and corrected, and the work needs a scope that explains what will be removed, cleaned, contained, and checked.

Harris County's flood-risk resources emphasize that risk varies by property and can differ from what a homeowner might infer from a single past storm or a FEMA map. The county's MAAPnext materials use updated rainfall, terrain, land-use, and hydrologic modeling. That makes property-specific investigation important. Flood exposure is not proof that a particular home has mold, and a mold claim should not be used as a shortcut to sell unnecessary demolition.

Texas regulates mold assessment and remediation through the Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR). The Texas Attorney General warns consumers about conflicts of interest: assessment and remediation generally must be handled by separate companies on the same project, with limited exceptions. The rules contain exemptions, including certain small projects and owner-performed work, so verify how the law applies to your exact situation.

## 1. Decide whether you need an assessment, remediation, or both

A mold assessor investigates and documents conditions, then prepares the assessment and remediation protocol where required. A remediator performs the cleanup under the applicable plan. Do not assume that the first contractor who offers a free inspection is qualified to define the problem and sell every step of the solution.

- **Ask:** *"Are you licensed by TDLR as a mold assessment company or a mold remediation company?"*
- **Ask:** *"Will you be doing both assessment and remediation on this project? If so, what specific legal exception applies?"*
- **Ask:** *"Who owns each company, and do the companies share owners or financial interests?"*
- **Request:** Company license numbers, the name and license number of the responsible person, and a written explanation of each company's role.

Texas generally prohibits the same company or commonly owned companies from performing both assessment and remediation on the same project, subject to statutory exceptions. The Texas Attorney General's consumer guidance explains this separation. Verify the current rule with TDLR rather than relying on a salesperson's summary.

## 2. Verify licenses and insurance directly

TDLR licenses mold assessment and remediation companies and the relevant individual license holders. TDLR's licensing materials state that licensed assessment and remediation companies must maintain at least $1 million in commercial general liability insurance per occurrence, subject to the agency's stated self-insurance provisions.

- **Ask:** *"Can I look up your active company license and responsible person in TDLR's online records?"*
- **Ask:** *"Will you provide a current certificate of insurance showing the named insured, policy dates, and coverage?"*
- **Ask:** *"Does your insurance cover the actual mold assessment or remediation work you are proposing?"*
- **Request:** The license details for the company and responsible person, plus a certificate of insurance sent directly by the agent if the project is substantial.

Do not treat a general contractor registration, a water-damage restoration certificate, or a trade association membership as a substitute for the Texas mold license when a license is required. Ask TDLR about exemptions if the work is a small area, routine maintenance, or another potentially exempt activity.

## 3. Require a project-specific written assessment and protocol

A useful assessment should identify the affected rooms and materials, explain the likely moisture source, and distinguish observed conditions from assumptions. For regulated work, the assessor's written protocol should be specific enough that you can compare bids and later determine whether the remediator followed it.

- **Ask:** *"Which rooms, surfaces, and materials are affected, and how did you determine the boundaries?"*
- **Ask:** *"What is the moisture source, and who will repair it?"*
- **Ask:** *"What materials will be removed, what will be cleaned, and what containment and protective equipment will be used?"*
- **Ask:** *"What are the clearance criteria, and who will verify that the work is complete?"*
- **Request:** The assessment report, photographs, moisture readings where taken, remediation protocol, and a room-by-room scope.

Be cautious when a contractor recommends removing large areas of drywall or flooring without explaining the evidence, the quantities, the containment plan, and the reason less destructive work is inadequate. Conversely, a promise to make mold disappear with a quick surface treatment may not address hidden contamination or the moisture source.

## 4. Compare bids by scope, containment, and exclusions

Ask each bidder to price the same protocol. One estimate may include demolition, bagging, disposal, cleaning, and final verification; another may price only labor or a limited visible area. Those are not comparable bids.

- **Ask:** *"What is included in containment, negative air or filtration equipment, removal, cleaning, disposal, and final cleanup?"*
- **Ask:** *"Who repairs the plumbing, roof, HVAC, drainage, or other moisture source, and is that work included?"*
- **Ask:** *"What is excluded, and what conditions would trigger a change order?"*
- **Ask:** *"Will you obtain my written approval before removing additional materials or increasing the price?"*
- **Request:** A fixed or clearly explained estimate, payment schedule, change-order terms, schedule, and warranty language.

If the work is connected to an insurance claim, review the proposed scope with your insurer before signing. The Texas Attorney General advises consumers to review bids with their insurer and reminds them that the insurer cannot require them to use a specific mold contractor.

## 5. Get the closeout records before making final payment

Texas rules require specific project documentation for regulated remediation. The Attorney General's consumer guidance says the remediator must provide a certificate of mold remediation within ten days after completing the work. That certificate includes an assessor's statement about compliance with the cleanup plan and indicates whether the underlying cause was corrected.

- **Ask:** *"Who will issue the certificate, and when will I receive it?"*
- **Ask:** *"Will the final file include before-and-after photographs, invoices, and the assessor's clearance statement?"*
- **Ask:** *"Does the certificate address the moisture source, or is that repair documented separately?"*
- **Request:** The final certificate, assessment and protocol, photographs, invoices, clearance documentation, and records of any separate water-source repairs.

Keep these records with your insurance and property documents. A mold-remediation certificate is not a warranty against every future moisture problem, and it does not certify unrelated structural or water-damage repairs.

## Warning signs before you book

- The contractor will not provide a TDLR license number when one is required.
- The same company insists it must both assess and remediate but cannot explain a legal exception.
- The quote is a single lump sum with no room-by-room scope or material quantities.
- The proposed work ignores the moisture source.
- The company promises a health outcome or permanent mold-free home.
- The contractor refuses to provide photographs, the remediation certificate, or final records.

## Before you book

- Document the water event and visible damage before work begins.
- Verify the applicable TDLR licenses and company insurance.
- Confirm whether assessment and remediation must be performed by separate companies.
- Get a written, project-specific protocol and compare bids against it.
- Clarify who fixes the leak or moisture source.
- Agree on clearance criteria, final documents, change orders, and payment milestones.
- Check current city, county, and state requirements for your address and project.

Houston is the focus of this checklist, but the property may be inside the City of Houston, another incorporated city, or unincorporated Harris County. Permit and floodplain requirements can differ. Harris County says certain work on properties in its Special Flood Hazard Area requires a residential permit and compliance with floodplain rules. Confirm the authority that has jurisdiction over your property before construction or substantial repairs. For mold licensing questions, use TDLR's current resources and the Texas Attorney General's consumer guidance.
