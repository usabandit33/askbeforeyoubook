Restaurants and food-service operators along Melbourne’s US-1 and Wickham corridors run high-grease cooking lines under constant inspection pressure. NFPA 96 is the national standard that fire authorities and insurers use for commercial cooking exhaust systems. If grease accumulation fails inspection, local fire marshals can shut the kitchen down and insurance coverage can be at risk. Many general cleaning companies only wipe visible surfaces; the fire risk lives in the filters, ducts, and rooftop fan.

Before you book, confirm the company cleans the entire exhaust path to bare metal on a schedule that matches your cooking volume—not just the shiny hood face.

## 1. Will you clean the full exhaust system, not only the hood face?

- **Ask:** *"Do you clean hoods, baffle filters, ductwork, and the rooftop exhaust fan to bare metal, and will you provide before-and-after photos of each section?"*
- **Red flag:** A quote that covers only filter swaps or a surface wipe of the canopy.

NFPA 96 addresses the entire grease exhaust system. Grease that remains in ducts or at the fan is still a fire load. Ask for photo documentation of the work so you have evidence for the fire marshal and your insurer.

## 2. What cleaning frequency applies to my cooking volume?

- **Ask:** *"Based on our menu and hours, what NFPA 96 frequency do you recommend—monthly, quarterly, semi-annually, or annually—and will you put that schedule in writing?"*
- **Red flag:** One generic interval for every restaurant regardless of charbroiling, wok cooking, or 24-hour operation.

NFPA 96 recommends more frequent cleaning for solid-fuel and high-volume operations, and longer intervals for low-volume kitchens. Local authorities having jurisdiction can require tighter schedules. Match the contract to your actual cooking style, not a one-size template.

## 3. Who is the authority having jurisdiction for my address?

- **Ask:** *"Which fire authority inspects this location—City of Melbourne, Brevard County, or another—and do you know their current documentation expectations?"*
- **Red flag:** A company that cannot name the local AHJ or produces only a generic receipt with no system details.

Inspection and recordkeeping expectations can vary by city and county. Ask the cleaner to list the date, areas cleaned, company name, and certification that the system was found satisfactory—information fire officials commonly request.

## 4. Is the crew trained, insured, and experienced with rooftop access?

- **Ask:** *"Are your technicians trained for NFPA 96-style exhaust cleaning, and does the price include safe rooftop access and fan service?"*
- **Red flag:** Subcontracted crews with no proof of insurance or no plan for the rooftop fan.

Rooftop fans and vertical ducts are part of the system. Confirm insurance, access method, and whether fan cleaning and reassembly are included. After-hours or emergency rates should be stated before you need them.

## 5. How does this interact with my suppression system and insurance?

- **Ask:** *"Will this cleaning coordinate with or avoid disturbing the fire-suppression system, and will the documentation support my insurance requirements?"*
- **Red flag:** Any suggestion that exhaust cleaning replaces required suppression inspection or that photos are unnecessary.

Hood cleaning and suppression system service are related but not identical. Keep cleaning certificates with your fire and insurance files. If an inspector or insurer asks for proof, you need dated, specific records—not a verbal assurance.

## Before you book

Require full-system cleaning to bare metal, a written frequency matched to your cooking volume, photo documentation, and clarity on the local fire authority’s expectations. Melbourne dining corridors cannot afford a failed inspection or a preventable grease fire.
