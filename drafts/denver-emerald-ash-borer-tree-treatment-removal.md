If you have an ash tree in Denver, the decision to treat it, remove it, or monitor it is no longer theoretical. Emerald ash borer (EAB) was confirmed in Denver in June 2025. The Colorado State Forest Service reported that Denver has more than 30,000 ash trees and that infested trees can die within two to four years after infestation. Other problems can resemble EAB damage, so a confident diagnosis matters before you spend money.

This is also a service where local rules are easy to overlook. Denver requires a city tree-service license for paid tree work, and removal of public or protected trees can require a permit. The city also has a limited ash removal and replacement program for some qualifying public-right-of-way trees. A homeowner who hires first and checks later may pay for work that needs approval, or miss a public program that could help.

Use these questions before hiring anyone to assess, treat, prune, or remove an ash tree.

## 1. Is this definitely an ash tree with emerald ash borer?

- **Ask:** *"What evidence makes you believe this is emerald ash borer, and can you show me the symptoms you found?"*

Request an on-site assessment, clear photographs, and a written explanation of the diagnosis. EAB signs can include D-shaped exit holes, canopy thinning, branch dieback, bark splitting, and galleries beneath the bark. Drought, root damage, other insects, and diseases can also cause decline. A sales representative pointing at a few dead branches is not a diagnosis.

If the evidence is uncertain, ask whether the City Forester or Colorado State University Extension should review the symptoms before you authorize expensive treatment or removal. The Colorado State Forest Service explains that EAB confirmation involves examination of samples by an entomologist, because lookalike pests and other causes of damage can be confusing.

## 2. Why treat this tree instead of removing it, or the other way around?

- **Ask:** *"What are the tree's condition, expected treatment schedule, and total cost over the next several years?"*

A qualified arborist should consider the tree's health, size, location, structural condition, infestation evidence, and the value of keeping it. Treatment may be worth discussing for a healthy, valuable ash tree in a suitable location. A severely declining or structurally unsafe tree may need a different plan.

Ask the contractor to explain:

- The treatment method and product proposed.
- Whether the treatment is preventive or intended for a tree with confirmed infestation.
- How often treatment must be repeated and what each visit costs.
- What signs would mean treatment is no longer worthwhile.
- Whether follow-up inspections are included.
- How the contractor will protect nearby plants, people, pets, and water features.

Do not accept a guarantee that a tree will live forever. Ask what the written warranty actually covers, how long it lasts, and what happens if the tree continues to decline.

## 3. Could the City of Denver program help before you pay?

- **Ask:** *"Is this tree in the public right-of-way, and does it qualify for Denver's Ash Removal and Replacement Program?"*

The public right-of-way is generally the area between the property line and the street, but the boundary varies. Denver recommends using the public tree inventory and property records to help identify a tree's location. If ownership is unclear, contact the Office of the City Forester before authorizing removal.

The city's program is limited. Its published eligibility criteria include ash trees that are dead or declining, no more than 12 inches in diameter, and located in the public right-of-way. The program schedule shown for 2026 covers west Denver. Eligibility and scheduling should be confirmed directly with the city because program capacity and annual plans can change.

The program does not mean the city will remove every ash tree or pay to treat trees on private property. Denver's EAB guidance says owners are responsible for ash trees on their own property. Check eligibility before signing a private contract for a tree that may be covered by a public program.

## 4. Is the tree company licensed by Denver?

- **Ask:** *"Can you show me the current Denver Tree Contractor License for the company and the individual license holder?"*

Denver's vegetation ordinance requires a Tree Contractor License for paid tree maintenance, including pruning and removal, within the City and County of Denver. Ask to see the license and verify the company on the city's current licensed contractor list.

The city says listed companies have met its minimum testing requirements, but it does not endorse them and warns that licensing does not guarantee quality. It recommends checking references and obtaining three to five detailed written bids.

For an assessment or treatment plan, you can also ask whether the person inspecting the tree is an ISA Certified Arborist. That credential is not a substitute for the Denver license, but it can help you evaluate the person's tree-care knowledge. Ask for the credential number and verify it through the [ISA credential verification directory](https://www.treesaregood.org/Find-an-Arborist/verifyacredential).

## 5. Who will apply any pesticide or injection treatment?

- **Ask:** *"Who will apply the treatment, what product and method will be used, and what applicator credentials apply?"*

If the proposal includes pesticide application, ask for the product name, label, target pest, application method, and the name of the person or subcontractor who will perform it. Ask whether the business or its treatment subcontractor holds the required Colorado commercial-applicator license for the work.

The City of Denver directs residents to the Colorado Department of Agriculture to check whether a tree company is also a licensed pesticide applicator. A company that only sells tree removal should not be allowed to blur that distinction when it quotes chemical treatment.

Do not approve a vague line item such as "EAB injection" without knowing what is being applied, who applies it, and whether repeat treatments are included.

## 6. What insurance protects you if something goes wrong?

- **Ask:** *"Can your insurance company send me current certificates for general liability and workers' compensation?"*

Tree work can involve heavy limbs, climbing, bucket trucks, cranes, chippers, roofs, fences, driveways, vehicles, and nearby utility lines. Ask for proof of general liability and workers' compensation coverage that applies to the actual work and crew.

Do not rely only on a certificate forwarded by the salesperson. Ask the insurance agent or carrier to confirm the policy is active and that the named business matches the company on your contract. Ask whether subcontractors will be used and who is responsible for their coverage.

## 7. What exactly will the crew do, and what is excluded?

- **Ask:** *"Can you itemize the work, cleanup, disposal, stump work, access needs, and any extra charges?"*

A removal proposal should state which tree is being removed, how the crew will protect nearby structures and landscaping, whether the stump will be ground, how deep the grinding will go, and whether all wood and debris will be hauled away. If the tree is close to a home, fence, overhead line, or neighboring property, ask how the crew will control the fall and protect the site.

For treatment, the proposal should state the tree's location and size, product, application method, number of trees, timing, repeat-treatment schedule, and follow-up inspection. If the company plans to move ash wood, ask how it will be handled to avoid spreading the pest. Denver advises residents not to transport ash firewood or other ash products out of the area.

## 8. Does the work need a permit?

- **Ask:** *"Have you checked whether this is a public, protected, or private tree, and who will obtain any required permit?"*

Denver requires an approved permit before removing public or protected trees. The city's current property-owner guidance says most trees on private property may be removed without a permit, but trees in front or side setbacks during demolition or construction are subject to City Forester oversight. The exact status of a tree and the property matters.

For public or protected trees, the contractor is responsible for obtaining the required City Forester permit before work begins. Ask for the permit number and written approval. Do not assume a verbal statement that "the homeowner can do what they want" is correct for a street tree.

## Before you book

- Confirm the tree species and ask for evidence supporting an EAB diagnosis.
- Compare treatment and removal options, including repeat treatment costs.
- Check the tree's location and public-program eligibility before paying.
- Verify the Denver Tree Contractor License.
- Verify ISA credentials if the company relies on them to support its expertise.
- Ask for insurance confirmation directly from the insurer.
- Get a written scope, cleanup plan, disposal plan, treatment details, warranty, and price.
- Confirm permits with Denver's Office of the City Forester before scheduling regulated work.

Denver's ash-tree situation is serious, but that does not mean every ash tree should be removed tomorrow. A careful assessment and a few detailed bids can help you protect the tree, your property, and your wallet. Verify current rules and program availability with the City and County of Denver before work begins.
