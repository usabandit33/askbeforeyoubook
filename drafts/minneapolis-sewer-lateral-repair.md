When a basement backs up, the most expensive mistake is often agreeing to excavation before you know where the problem is, who owns each section of pipe, and whether city programs affect the job. In Minneapolis, homeowners are responsible for private sanitary sewer laterals, including the portion that may extend into the public right of way. The city also offers grant and assessment pathways with specific eligibility rules, bid requirements, and repayment consequences.

That makes this a service where a “just get it fixed today” decision can accidentally erase funding options or turn a repair into a property-tax obligation.

## 1. Confirm the diagnosis before authorizing digging

- **Ask:** Did you run a camera from the house to the city main, and can I have the video?
- **Request:** The written report, footage with timestamps or distance markers, photographs, and the location of the defect.
- **Ask:** Is the problem a blockage, root intrusion, collapse, separated joint, bellied pipe, or foundation drain connection?

A vague statement such as “the line is bad” is not enough for a major excavation quote. Camera evidence should let you compare whether cleaning, spot repair, trenchless work, or full replacement is appropriate. Ask whether the camera reached the public main or stopped at an obstruction. A stopped camera can explain uncertainty, but it should not be disguised as certainty.

## 2. Separate the scope you need from the extras you do not

Minneapolis programs may cover sanitary sewer lateral repair and replacement, certain foundation drain disconnections, and inspection or cleaning costs when they lead to an eligible repair. They generally do not cover unrelated indoor plumbing, tree removal, or landscaping.

- **Ask:** Which line segment is included in this proposal?
- **Request:** A marked site plan, excavation length and depth, restoration assumptions, traffic or right-of-way work, and a list of excluded costs.
- **Ask:** What happens if the contractor finds a second defect after the ground is open?

Get separate prices for diagnosis, cleaning, repair, replacement, permits, traffic control, pavement or sidewalk restoration, landscaping, and interior restoration. A quote that looks cheap because half the project is hidden in “allowances” is not a cheap quote. It is a suspense novel with your checking account as the villain.

## 3. Check Minneapolis assistance before signing away your options

The City of Minneapolis private sewer lateral grant program requires eligible owner-occupied properties, proof that a repair is needed, and at least three bids from approved contractors. The city also has an assessment program that can allow eligible owners to repay costs through property taxes, with a $50 administrative fee and interest rates that vary by term and year.

- **Ask:** Is my property eligible for the current grant or assessment program?
- **Request:** A contractor report suitable for the city application, three comparable bids, and copies of the program forms.
- **Ask:** If I accept an assessment, what would the repayment schedule and total cost be?

Do not assume a program is open forever. The 2026 grant application window was limited, and the city states that funding ends when available money is exhausted. Confirm current deadlines and eligibility directly with Minneapolis Public Works before work begins.

## 4. Verify permits and contractor responsibility

Minneapolis requires permits for private utility work in public areas, including certain sewer work and excavation. Ask who is pulling each permit and who will respond if the city inspector requires a correction.

- **Request:** Permit numbers, proof of insurance, contractor license or registration information applicable to the work, and the name of the person supervising the crew.
- **Ask:** Who restores the street, sidewalk, boulevard, and landscaping?
- **Warning sign:** A contractor who says permit responsibility is “your problem” while also controlling the excavation scope.

If the project crosses a right of way, utility locates and public-area requirements matter. Ask for the expected schedule and whether weather, pavement moratoriums, or utility conflicts could change the completion date.

## 5. Put warranty and payment terms in plain English

- **Ask:** What exactly is warranted, for how long, and who pays if the repaired segment fails?
- **Request:** Written warranty exclusions, lien waivers or equivalent payment documentation, and a final invoice that matches the approved scope.
- **Avoid:** Full payment before the line is tested, the permit is closed, and the surface is restored.

For large jobs, tie payments to observable milestones: accepted diagnosis, permitted excavation, completed repair, successful inspection or test, and restoration.

## Warning signs

- No camera footage or no written location of the defect.
- Pressure to start digging before you check city assistance.
- A proposal that combines sewer repair with unrelated work and one lump sum.
- “Guaranteed” grant eligibility without confirmation from Minneapolis.
- No answer about who owns or restores work in the public right of way.
- Contractor requests a large cash deposit and refuses a detailed contract.
- The final invoice adds footage, disposal, restoration, or permit charges that were never explained.

## Before you book

- [ ] Save the camera video and written diagnosis.
- [ ] Confirm the exact repair segment and whether the public right of way is involved.
- [ ] Check current Minneapolis grant and assessment eligibility.
- [ ] Get at least three comparable written bids if a city program requires them.
- [ ] Confirm permit, utility-locate, inspection, and restoration responsibilities.
- [ ] Read warranty, change-order, and payment terms before authorizing excavation.
- [ ] Keep copies of every report, permit, bid, invoice, and photo.

Sewer work is urgent, but urgency is not a permission slip for bad paperwork. Make the contractor prove the problem, define the scope, and explain the city rules before the shovel goes in.
