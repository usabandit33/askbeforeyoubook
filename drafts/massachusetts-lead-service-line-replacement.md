Massachusetts homeowners may receive a notice that a property has a lead service line, a galvanized line, or an unknown material. The next question is not simply "How much does replacement cost?" It is "Which part of the line do I own, which part does the water supplier own, and what work is actually required?"

MassDEP explains that ownership and payment can depend on the town and public water supplier. In some arrangements, the homeowner owns the portion from the curb stop to the home and may be responsible for that portion. A partial replacement can also create different sampling and communication obligations than a full replacement.

## 1. Confirm the line material and ownership

Ask:

- "How was the service-line material identified?"
- "Do you have an inventory record for my address?"
- "Where is the public-private ownership boundary?"
- "Who owns the curb stop, gooseneck, and fittings?"
- "Is the recommendation full replacement or partial replacement?"

Request the utility's written notice, inventory record, photographs, and any prior water test results. Do not rely on a contractor's guess based on the age of the house. The material, ownership, and replacement scope should be documented before excavation begins.

## 2. Understand sampling and health information

Ask the water supplier or contractor:

- "Do I need a certified laboratory test before deciding?"
- "What sample protocol applies?"
- "If the line is partially replaced, what post-work sampling is required?"
- "What filters or interim precautions are recommended?"

MassDEP says a partial replacement can require a representative lead sample within 72 hours after the work. That is one reason a homeowner should not let a contractor improvise the testing plan. Ask who will collect the sample, which laboratory will analyze it, and who receives the report.

A single test is not a guarantee that future water quality will never change. Keep the lab report, chain-of-custody information, and post-work instructions.

## 3. Verify the contractor, permits, and street work

Ask:

- "What Massachusetts license or registration authorizes this work?"
- "Who pulls plumbing, excavation, and public-way permits?"
- "Who coordinates utility locates and traffic control?"
- "Who restores pavement, sidewalks, landscaping, and interior finishes?"
- "Who schedules the final inspection?"

Request current insurance, references for lead-service-line projects, and a written restoration scope. Street work can involve several agencies and subcontractors. If the bid does not identify who pays for unexpected rock, utility conflicts, or restoration, the final price is not yet knowable.

## 4. Compare full, partial, and alternative scopes

A written proposal should explain the difference between:

- Full replacement from the water main to the building.
- Replacement of only the private portion.
- Replacement of a gooseneck or fitting.
- Temporary treatment and monitoring while funding is arranged.
- Work performed by the public water supplier or a municipal program.

Ask what happens if the public side is replaced but the private side is not. Ask whether the utility, town, or grant program requires preapproval. Massachusetts consumer guidance says cost responsibility can vary substantially by ownership arrangement, so a contractor should not promise that the town will pay or that the homeowner must pay everything without documentation.

## 5. Check funding and contract protections

Ask whether your community has a loan, grant, cost-share, or coordinated replacement program. Request written eligibility rules and deadlines. Do not begin work that may disqualify you from assistance without confirming the program's preapproval requirements.

The contract should identify the exact pipe length, materials, permits, testing, restoration, payment schedule, change-order process, and warranty. Avoid cash-only arrangements or large deposits before the utility confirms the scope.

## Warning signs

- The contractor cannot explain ownership boundaries.
- The bid is based only on a visual guess.
- Partial replacement is proposed without a post-work sampling plan.
- The company promises a municipal grant without written eligibility.
- Permits and street restoration are excluded.
- The contractor refuses to identify the laboratory or license.
- The contract says "replace lead line" without defining the endpoints.

## Before you book

Start with the water supplier's written inventory and ownership information. Compare at least three bids against the same scope, verify licenses and insurance, confirm permits and restoration, and ask about sampling after the work. Verify current Massachusetts and local water-supplier requirements before signing because federal and state service-line rules, inventories, and funding programs can change.
