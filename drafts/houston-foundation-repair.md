Houston homes move. Expansive clay soils, drainage problems, plumbing leaks, tree roots, and normal seasonal changes can all produce cracks or uneven floors. That does not mean every crack requires piers, hydraulic lifting, or a five-figure repair package. Before you hire a foundation company, make sure the proposed fix is based on evidence rather than a dramatic sales pitch.

Texas consumer guidance warns homeowners to get multiple written bids, avoid pressure, understand lien language, and keep payment tied to completed work. Those basic protections matter even more when a foundation contractor is recommending work that can affect plumbing, doors, floors, drainage, and resale documentation.

## 1. Ask what has actually been measured

- **Ask:** “What evidence shows movement, what is the cause, and what measurement will prove the repair worked?”
- **Request:** A written inspection report, floor-elevation readings or other measurements, crack photographs, drainage observations, plumbing-leak findings, and the contractor's repair assumptions.
- **Why it matters:** A crack map is not the same as a diagnosis. Ask whether the pattern suggests settlement, heave, moisture movement, structural damage, or a non-structural finish issue.

If a contractor recommends piers after a ten-minute walk-through with no measurements, slow down. A second opinion from a licensed professional engineer may cost less than a wrong repair.

## 2. Separate diagnosis from sales

Ask every bidder:

- Do you employ or retain a licensed professional engineer?
- Will the engineer provide a stamped or signed report?
- What portion of the work is engineering, excavation, plumbing, concrete, drainage, and cosmetic repair?
- What conditions would change the price?
- What work is excluded?

A fair comparison requires equal scope. One bid may include drainage improvements and plumbing repair while another prices only pier installation. The cheapest number can be the most expensive quote if it leaves the cause untouched.

## 3. Understand Houston permitting and site logistics

Ask who will confirm whether a permit, inspection, right-of-way approval, or other city requirement applies to your exact address and scope. Foundation work can involve excavation, structural changes, plumbing, concrete, drainage, and access for heavy equipment. Get the answer in writing.

Also ask how the contractor will protect:

- Driveways and sidewalks
- Neighboring fences and landscaping
- Gas, water, sewer, and electrical lines
- Interior finishes and flooring
- Children, pets, and occupied rooms

A foundation proposal that ignores access, utility locating, spoil removal, and restoration is incomplete.

## 4. Read the warranty like a lawyer, not a believer

Foundation warranties often sound impressive until you read the exclusions. Ask:

- Is the warranty transferable?
- Does it cover workmanship, materials, movement, plumbing damage, and water intrusion?
- Does it require annual inspections or paid maintenance?
- Who performs warranty adjustments?
- Are drainage or plumbing repairs conditions of coverage?
- What happens if the company closes or is sold?

If the warranty promises “lifetime protection,” ask whose lifetime, under what conditions, and what proof is required to make a claim. A warranty that depends on maintaining a system nobody explained is not much of a warranty.

## 5. Protect the contract and payments

Texas consumer guidance warns that home-improvement contracts can create lien risk and recommends written scope, clear payment terms, and caution about paying in full before completion. Ask for:

- A signed contract with no blanks
- A detailed scope and unit prices
- Start and completion expectations
- Change-order rules
- Proof of general liability and workers' compensation coverage
- Lien waivers or releases from the contractor and major suppliers
- A final payment tied to completion, cleanup, documentation, and inspection

Never sign a blank certificate of completion. Keep copies of the contract, engineering report, permit records, elevation data, invoices, photos, and warranty.

## Warning signs

- The salesperson diagnoses a major failure before measuring anything.
- You are told every competitor is dishonest and you must sign today.
- The bid uses “as needed” without a price limit.
- The contractor refuses to identify the engineer or insurance carrier.
- The warranty excludes the very movement or water problem you hired them to solve.
- You are asked to pay in full before the work starts.
- The contractor will not explain lien paperwork or utility protection.

## Before you book

- Get at least three written scopes and compare equal work.
- Consider an independent engineering opinion for expensive or structural repairs.
- Verify insurance and permit responsibility.
- Ask how the contractor will document baseline and post-repair measurements.
- Put every promise, material, exclusion, and change-order rule in the contract.
- Do not release final payment until the site is safe, clean, documented, and inspected.
