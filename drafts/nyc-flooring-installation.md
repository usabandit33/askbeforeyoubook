New flooring looks like a straightforward purchase until the installer arrives and the quote grows. The advertised price may cover the flooring and a basic installation, but not moving furniture, removing old material, stairs, subfloor leveling, disposal, transitions, door trimming, or work around radiators and built-in cabinets. In a New York City apartment, building access rules, elevator reservations, delivery windows, and noise restrictions can add another layer of cost.

This is a promising local hiring decision because New York City has both a clear licensing framework and documented consumer harm in the flooring category. In February 2025, the NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP) announced a settlement requiring National Floors Direct to provide $250,000 in consumer restitution and pay $100,000 in civil penalties. The city said its investigation found nearly 2,500 counts of alleged violations, including misleading installation timing, undisclosed additional charges, poor workmanship, and refund or cancellation problems. The company had been one of the city's most complained-about home improvement contractors over the preceding five years. This enforcement action concerns one company and does not establish that all flooring installers behave this way. It does show why a customer should insist on a detailed scope and clear cancellation terms.

## 1. Verify the contractor's NYC home improvement license

- **Ask:** *"What is the exact legal business name and NYC Home Improvement Contractor license number that will appear on my contract?"*
- **Check:** Search the business through NYC DCWP's license lookup before paying or signing.
- **Request:** A copy of the license, proof of general liability insurance, workers' compensation coverage where required, and the name of any subcontractor who will perform the installation.

NYC requires a Home Improvement Contractor license for covered work costing more than $200 at a residential building. Flooring installation is a residential improvement, and a company should not use a different business name or a salesperson's credentials to avoid the licensing question. The city's license rules exclude certain separately regulated trades, so if the project includes electrical or plumbing work, verify those trades separately.

A DCWP license also matters because eligible customers may have access to the city's Home Improvement Contractor Trust Fund if a licensed contractor owes them money. Do not assume the fund covers every loss or every dispute. Ask DCWP what protection applies to your situation, and never hire an unlicensed company on the assumption that the price savings are worth it.

## 2. Make the quote spell out every part of the installation

- **Ask:** *"Is this a complete installed price, and exactly what work is excluded?"*
- **Request:** A room-by-room scope with product name, manufacturer, model or SKU, thickness, wear layer or finish, underlayment, installation method, trim, transitions, and total square footage.

A useful estimate should separately identify:

- Flooring material and the quantity ordered, including waste allowance.
- Delivery, building access, elevator or stair carry, and storage.
- Removal of existing carpet, tile, hardwood, or laminate.
- Disposal and hauling fees.
- Subfloor inspection, moisture testing, leveling, patching, or replacement.
- Underlayment, vapor barrier, adhesive, fasteners, and transition strips.
- Baseboards, shoe molding, door trimming, and appliance movement.
- Furniture moving, protection of adjacent rooms, cleanup, and final walkthrough.

Do not accept a broad line such as "standard installation included" without a written definition. Ask what the installer considers standard and what conditions trigger an extra charge. If the existing floor may contain asbestos, stop and arrange the appropriate inspection before authorizing removal. Flooring work in older buildings can disturb suspect materials, and the installer should not simply grind, sand, or tear them out without addressing the hazard.

## 3. Ask how the installer will inspect the subfloor

- **Ask:** *"What conditions will you inspect before installation, and how will you price repairs if the subfloor is uneven, damp, or damaged?"*

The new floor can fail even when the material itself is sound. Uneven subfloors can create movement or gaps. Moisture can cause some wood and laminate products to swell or cup. A concrete slab may need a moisture test and a specific adhesive or underlayment. Older apartments can have multiple flooring layers that change the finished height at doorways and appliances.

Ask the installer to inspect the actual rooms and document existing conditions before the material is delivered. Request moisture readings where relevant, the manufacturer's installation requirements, and the tolerances the crew will use to decide whether the subfloor is ready. If the contractor discovers a problem after removing the old floor, require photos and a written change order with a price before the extra work begins.

## 4. Confirm the product is suitable for the room and building

- **Ask:** *"What exact product are you installing, and can you show me the manufacturer's technical sheet and warranty?"*

A product suitable for a dry bedroom may not be the right choice for a kitchen, entry, laundry area, or basement. Ask about water resistance, scratch and dent performance, sunlight exposure, cleaning products, sound transmission, and required expansion gaps. If you live in a co-op or condominium, ask management whether there are acoustic underlayment or impact-sound requirements before ordering material.

Request the manufacturer, product line, color, SKU, lot or batch information, and written warranty. Confirm whether the warranty covers material only or also labor, and whether it excludes moisture, improper cleaning, subfloor conditions, or installation outside the manufacturer's instructions. Keep a sample and the packaging label until the job is accepted.

## 5. Put NYC contract and cancellation protections in writing

- **Ask:** *"Will you provide the complete contract and required cancellation notice before collecting a deposit?"*

New York State requires a written home-improvement contract for covered work over $500. The contract should identify the contractor, describe the work and materials, state the price and approximate start and completion dates, and explain the right to cancel within three business days, subject to the law's rules and exceptions. State law also requires protections for payments made before completion, including escrow or bond requirements. Ask the contractor to explain in writing how your payments are protected.

Do not sign a blank or incomplete contract. Make sure the legal business name and license number match the company you verified. The payment schedule should track work completed and materials delivered, not require the entire price before the crew arrives. If a salesperson says you cannot cancel, or that a deposit is automatically forfeited regardless of the law and contract, pause and get independent guidance.

## 6. Plan for apartment-building logistics

- **Ask:** *"Have you confirmed my building's contractor access, insurance certificate, elevator reservation, work hours, and material delivery rules?"*

Many NYC buildings require a certificate of insurance (COI) with specific wording, coverage limits, and additional-insured parties. Ask management for the exact requirements before scheduling. Give the installer the rules and get written confirmation that the company can meet them. Clarify who pays for an elevator reservation, missed delivery, parking, or a return trip if the building refuses entry because paperwork is incomplete.

Ask whether the installer will protect common areas, remove debris through an approved route, and keep hallways clear. A quote that excludes building requirements may be cheaper on paper but more expensive once the job is scheduled.

## 7. Define the final walkthrough and warranty

- **Ask:** *"What defects will you correct before final payment, and how do I request warranty service?"*

Before the final payment, inspect seams, transitions, trim, thresholds, stair edges, door clearances, and areas around appliances. Check for hollow or moving sections, visible gaps, uneven edges, adhesive residue, scratches, and damage to walls or cabinets. Photograph any issue and put the correction list in writing.

Get separate written warranty terms for the flooring product and installation labor. Ask who handles a claim if the manufacturer blames installation and the installer blames the product. Keep the final invoice, product labels, care instructions, moisture or subfloor records, and any building approval.

## Warning signs

- The business will not provide a verifiable NYC license number.
- The quote uses "free installation" or "standard installation" without defining what is included.
- Removal, disposal, subfloor preparation, or transitions are priced only after you sign.
- The installer will not identify the product SKU or provide the manufacturer's warranty.
- The company demands full payment upfront or asks you to sign a blank contract.
- The salesperson pressures you to waive cancellation rights or says a written contract is unnecessary.
- The installer plans to disturb old flooring without addressing possible asbestos.
- No one can explain who obtains building approval, provides the COI, or handles damage to common areas.

## Before you book

- Verify the contractor's exact legal name and NYC DCWP license.
- Compare at least two itemized quotes using the same product and room measurements.
- Confirm removal, disposal, subfloor preparation, trim, transitions, and building fees.
- Review the product data sheet and both material and labor warranties.
- Obtain the complete contract and cancellation notice before paying.
- Confirm building access, insurance, elevator, and work-hour requirements.
- Keep photos, change orders, receipts, and the final walkthrough list.

NYC's licensing and consumer-protection rules are specific to the city. If the property is outside the five boroughs, verify the contractor requirements and home-improvement contract rules for that municipality and county. Confirm current licensing, cancellation, payment-protection, and permit requirements with DCWP and the New York Attorney General before work begins.
