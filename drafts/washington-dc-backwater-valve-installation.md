Washington, DC homeowners with below-grade plumbing or a history of basement flooding may be considering a backwater valve, sump-pump work, grading changes, or other flood-resilience upgrades. The project sounds simple until you learn that the valve may be installed under a basement slab, in a yard, or on a sewer lateral that must be coordinated with local plumbing rules.

DC Water says a properly working backwater valve allows wastewater to flow out of the building but not back in during sewer surcharges or storm-related backups. In some locations, the installation is also required when plumbing fixtures sit below the top of the upstream street manhole.

## 1. Start with the source of the water

Ask:

- “Is the problem a sewer backup, overland flooding, foundation seepage, or a combination?”
- “What evidence shows the sewer lateral is the right place for a valve?”
- “Has the line been camera-inspected?”
- “Does the property already have a sump pump or backflow device?”

Request videos, photos, flood history, insurance claims information, and any prior plumbing permits. A contractor who treats every basement leak as a backwater-valve problem may be selling the device before diagnosing the path of entry.

Use the DC Flood Risk Tool and the city’s flood-resilience resources to understand whether the property has floodplain, stormwater, or topographic risk. A valve cannot stop water entering through a window well or foundation crack.

## 2. Clarify the exact installation

Ask the contractor to show where the valve will go and what will be opened or removed. The quote should identify:

- Interior or exterior installation.
- Slab cutting and concrete replacement.
- Excavation depth.
- Sewer-lateral diameter and material.
- Cleanout access.
- Bypass or maintenance access.
- Pump, alarm, or battery backup.
- Restoration of flooring, walls, landscaping, and utilities.

Ask who will operate and maintain the valve after installation. A device that is buried behind a finished wall or installed without practical access may become an expensive decoration.

## 3. Verify licensing, permits, and inspection

Ask:

- “What DC plumbing license authorizes this work?”
- “Who will pull the permit?”
- “Will the job require a public-space or right-of-way permit?”
- “Who schedules the inspection?”
- “What happens if the inspector requires a change?”

Request the contractor’s license information, insurance certificate, permit number, and written warranty. Do not allow a contractor to claim that a below-grade sewer modification is “too small to permit” without checking the current District rules.

If the installation affects a shared sewer line, adjoining property, or public connection, ask which approvals are required before excavation begins.

## 4. Check DC Water programs before signing

DC Water has an Expanded Backwater Valve Program and a Floodproofing Rebate Program in selected drainage areas. The floodproofing program can reimburse eligible residents up to $3,000 for approved measures, but eligibility, consultation, and preapproval requirements matter.

Ask:

- “Is my address inside an eligible program area?”
- “Must I have a homeowner consultation before work starts?”
- “Does the program require an approved device or contractor?”
- “Will starting work before approval make me ineligible?”
- “What costs are excluded?”

Never let a contractor promise a rebate without showing the current program rules. Confirm directly with DC Water.

## 5. Compare the whole cost

Compare proposals using the same scope. Include camera inspection, engineering or layout work, excavation, slab cutting, valve and fittings, pump and alarm equipment, permits, inspections, restoration, cleanup, and maintenance instructions.

Ask whether the quote is for one valve or a larger drainage package. Some homes need a sump pump, grading work, drain improvements, or insurance changes as well. Those may be worthwhile, but they should not be smuggled into the project as surprise add-ons.

## Warning signs

- No camera inspection or explanation of the water source.
- No license or permit information.
- A rebate is promised without address verification.
- The quote does not explain access and maintenance.
- The valve is installed with no alarm, cleanout, or service plan.
- Slab, flooring, or yard restoration is excluded.
- The contractor promises the valve will prevent every type of flooding.
- A large deposit is demanded before a written scope is provided.

## Before you book

Confirm the source of the water, obtain a camera inspection, verify the contractor and permit path, and check DC Water’s current programs before signing. Ask for before-and-after photos, test results, the inspection record, and written maintenance instructions.

Verify current District of Columbia plumbing, floodproofing, and DC Water requirements before signing. Water has a way of finding every weak point, including weak contracts.
