A private well makes you responsible for decisions that a public water utility normally handles: testing, understanding the results, and arranging treatment when needed. On Cape Cod, that responsibility deserves attention. Barnstable County says private wells serve nearly 20% of Cape Cod households. The county also explains that the region's sandy soils and connected aquifer can allow contaminants from surface and underground sources to reach groundwater.

This does not mean every Cape Cod well is contaminated. It means a homeowner should not choose a filter based on a salesperson's guess, a water taste, or a vague promise of a "complete water test."

The county's 2025 free private-well testing program offered screening for bacteria, nitrates, metals, PFAS, and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). The county's current program page says the available sampling allocations in all towns have been filled. If you still need a test, compare the available laboratory options and confirm current fees and turnaround times.

Use these questions before hiring a testing service or a company to install water treatment.

## 1. Is the home actually supplied by a private well?

- **Ask:** *"Can you confirm whether this property uses a private well, a public water connection, or both?"*

Do not assume that a rural-looking property has a private well or that a home with a treatment tank is on well water. Ask the seller, property manager, or local water department and check the property's records. If the home has more than one source, identify which taps are supplied by each system.

If you are buying a home, request the latest laboratory reports, well construction and repair records, pump information, and any treatment-system service history. A recent report from the seller is useful, but it does not replace your own testing if the report is incomplete, old, or based on a different test panel.

## 2. Which contaminants will the lab test for?

- **Ask:** *"Can you give me the exact analyte list, laboratory methods, and price before I pay?"*

A generic "full panel" label does not tell you what is included. Ask whether the proposed test covers bacteria, nitrate, sodium, pH, conductance, metals, VOCs, and PFAS. The appropriate panel depends on the well's history, location, nearby land uses, prior results, and local Board of Health requirements.

Barnstable County's private-well program page recommends annual testing for at least coliform bacteria, pH, specific conductance, sodium, and nitrates, with more comprehensive testing for copper, iron, manganese, and VOCs every three years. The county's current laboratory page lists iron, copper, and manganese in its routine annual test kit, so compare the actual analyte list rather than relying on the word routine. The county also says PFAS testing should be considered for drinking-water wells, especially when a well is within one to two miles of a known PFAS source or an area where PFAS has been detected.

MassDEP's Private Well Guidelines recommend annual testing for total coliform and nitrate/nitrite, with other contaminants tested initially and then every ten years. A local Board of Health may require more frequent or additional testing. Barnstable County's more frequent local recommendations reflect Cape Cod conditions. Ask your town's health agent which schedule applies to your property.

## 3. Is the laboratory certified for the tests you need?

- **Ask:** *"Is the laboratory currently MassDEP-certified for each contaminant in this panel, and can I verify that certification?"*

MassDEP strongly encourages homeowners to use a state-certified laboratory for private drinking-water analysis. Certification is specific to the tests a lab is approved to perform. Do not assume that a lab certified for bacteria is also certified for PFAS or every metal.

Use MassDEP's certified-laboratory search to check the lab and the relevant analytes. Barnstable County announced in March 2025 that its Water Quality Laboratory had earned MassDEP certification for PFAS analysis and was one of five Massachusetts labs then equipped to perform that testing.

Ask whether the lab performs the analysis itself or sends samples to another laboratory. If work is subcontracted, ask which lab will issue the final report and whether that lab has the necessary certification.

## 4. Who collects the sample, and how will contamination be prevented?

- **Ask:** *"Who supplies the bottles, collects the sample, completes the chain-of-custody form, and delivers it to the lab?"*

Poor sampling can produce misleading results. Ask for written instructions before collecting anything yourself. Find out whether the lab requires a trained sampler, a particular tap, a flushing period, special bottles, cooling, or a specific delivery window.

If the goal is to understand the untreated well water, the sample generally needs to come from a tap before the treatment equipment. Barnstable County specifically advises using untreated water rather than a tap after a filter or treatment system. If you want to test whether an existing filter is working, ask the lab to design a separate post-treatment sample plan.

Get the chain-of-custody record and a copy of the complete laboratory report. Do not rely on a salesperson's verbal summary of the results.

## 5. What will the report tell you?

- **Ask:** *"Will the report show the result, units, detection limit, applicable standard or guideline, and recommended next step for every analyte?"*

A useful report identifies what was tested, the measured concentration, the units, the laboratory method, and the applicable comparison value. Ask the lab to explain any result that is above a drinking-water standard or guideline, and ask whether a confirmation sample is appropriate before making a major purchase.

The U.S. Geological Survey studied tap water from 20 Cape Cod residences, including 10 private and 10 public supplies, and found multiple regulated and unregulated chemicals in the samples. That study is a small research sample, not a survey showing how many Cape Cod wells are contaminated. It is useful evidence that testing at the tap can identify substances that homeowners cannot detect by sight, smell, or taste.

## 6. Is a treatment system actually needed?

- **Ask:** *"Which specific result is this system designed to address, and what independent performance evidence supports that claim?"*

Do not agree to a whole-house system just because a salesperson says the water is "bad." Ask the company to connect each proposed treatment stage to a specific contaminant in your laboratory report. Request the model numbers, treatment technology, rated flow, maintenance schedule, replacement-media costs, and the contaminant-specific performance data.

Ask whether the system is designed for the whole house or only drinking and cooking water. Ask what happens to backwash or wastewater, whether the system changes water pressure, and whether any plumbing changes or permits are required. The treatment installer should explain the limits of the equipment, not promise that one filter removes everything.

Massachusetts guidance places responsibility for private-well water quality and treatment-system design, installation, monitoring, and maintenance on the well owner. Ask your local Board of Health whether it has requirements for treatment equipment, plumbing changes, or follow-up testing.

## 7. Who will install and maintain the equipment?

- **Ask:** *"Who will do the plumbing, what credentials or permits are required for this work, and who services the system after installation?"*

A company that sells treatment equipment may use a separate plumbing contractor. Identify that contractor in the proposal. If the work changes plumbing, ask the town's building department whether a licensed plumber and permit are required for the specific scope.

Get a written installation plan, itemized price, warranty, service contact, and schedule for replacing cartridges, membranes, resin, or other media. Ask how the company will verify that the treatment system is working after installation. A post-installation laboratory test is more useful than a salesperson saying the water tastes better.

## 8. Are you comparing the same tests and services?

- **Ask:** *"Can you compare the total price, analytes, sampling method, turnaround time, and follow-up support line by line?"*

Barnstable County currently publishes prices of $70 for its routine well analysis, $190 for its real-estate package, and $265 for its PFAS-18 analysis. Those are county laboratory prices, not a guarantee of what every private lab will charge. Confirm the current fee and exactly what is included before booking.

Be cautious if a testing company refuses to identify the laboratory, cannot provide the analyte list, or uses a test visit mainly to sell a treatment package. A separate testing step can make it easier to evaluate results before deciding what equipment, if any, is justified.

## Before you book

- Confirm whether the property has a private well and obtain its available history.
- Ask your local Board of Health about current testing rules and recommended schedules.
- Compare exact analyte lists, laboratory methods, certification, sample handling, and total fees.
- Test untreated water when the goal is to understand the well itself.
- Get the full laboratory report before agreeing to treatment equipment.
- Require the installer to link each treatment stage to a measured contaminant.
- Get installation, plumbing, maintenance, warranty, and post-installation testing details in writing.

A good well-water company should be willing to explain the test before selling the fix. Verify current laboratory certification, local Board of Health requirements, and installation permits with MassDEP and your town before making a decision.
