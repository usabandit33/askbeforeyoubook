Adding a battery to an existing solar system sounds straightforward: store extra electricity, use it later, and keep selected circuits running during an outage. In practice, the project can involve a new inverter or gateway, electrical-panel changes, backup-load design, utility interconnection, fire and building requirements, rate-plan changes, and a new set of warranties. The battery's advertised capacity is not the same as the amount of power your home can use at once or the number of hours it can run during an outage.

Sacramento is a useful market because Sacramento Municipal Utility District (SMUD) operates a local battery-storage program with incentives and a distinct Solar and Storage Rate. SMUD's homeowner page says its upfront enrollment incentive changed on September 23, 2026 from $500 per kWh, up to $10,000, to $300 per kWh, up to $6,000. The higher incentive required interconnection submission by September 22 and enrollment by December 31, 2026. As of this article's review date, November 25, 2026, the lower incentive is the current published amount for qualifying new submissions. Eligibility and funding terms can change, so confirm them directly with SMUD before signing.

There is also a wider consumer-risk signal. California's Contractors State License Board reported in its 2025 Accomplishments and Activities Report that it continued to receive about 177 solar-related complaints per month in 2025. The agency said many involved solar companies abandoning projects after receiving payment for work not performed. It reported more than $3.5 million in restitution from settled solar-related cases that year. Those figures cover California's solar sector generally; they are not battery-only complaints and are not specific to Sacramento. They still make license checks, written scope, and milestone payments especially important.

## 1. Start with your existing solar contract and utility rate

- **Ask:** *"What rate am I on today, what changes if I add a battery, and what incentive conditions apply to my account?"*
- **Request:** Existing solar agreement, permission-to-operate record, utility bills, interconnection approval, system diagram, and written SMUD rate-and-incentive explanation.

SMUD says existing solar customers on its older Net Energy Metering (NEM) rate can generally remain on that rate until December 31, 2030, but customers who choose the battery incentive and move to the Solar and Storage Rate may have a different bill structure. SMUD also describes circumstances where increasing existing solar generation beyond specified thresholds can require a new interconnection application and a move to the Solar and Storage Rate.

Ask the installer to show estimated bill impact using your actual usage and current rate. Do not rely on a generic savings estimate or a promise that the battery "pays for itself." Compare the value of backup power, time-of-use shifting, program incentives, financing costs, and any changed export credits.

## 2. Verify the contractor and electrical qualifications

- **Ask:** *"Which California contractor license classifications cover the battery, electrical panel, inverter, and any solar changes, and who will perform each part?"*
- **Request:** Active CSLB license number, proof of insurance, workers' compensation information, named electrical subcontractor, and license numbers of every business that will contract directly with you.
- **Check:** Search the Contractors State License Board license lookup and review complaints or disciplinary history where available.

A battery retrofit is not just a product delivery. It can involve electrical service equipment, wiring, transfer equipment, grounding, and changes to the existing PV system. Make sure the business named in the contract has the proper license scope and that any separate electrical contractor is identified. Ask who is responsible for the permit, utility application, inspections, commissioning, and correction of failed inspections.

## 3. Make the backup design specific

- **Ask:** *"Which circuits will remain powered during an outage, what is the continuous and peak output, and how long should the system run under my expected loads?"*
- **Request:** One-line electrical diagram, backup-load schedule, battery model and usable capacity, inverter output ratings, equipment locations, and written explanation of what will not work during an outage.

A battery's kilowatt-hour rating describes stored energy, not maximum power available at one moment. A system may have enough energy for lights and a refrigerator but not enough output to start an air conditioner, well pump, or other large motor. Ask whether the design backs up the whole home or selected circuits. If the installer claims whole-home backup, ask for the load calculation and equipment needed to support it.

Also ask what happens when the battery is depleted, whether solar can recharge it during an outage, whether the system can operate without internet, and how the backup gateway interacts with your existing inverter.

## 4. Confirm Sacramento permits and SMUD interconnection

- **Ask:** *"Who obtains the building and electrical permits, submits the SMUD interconnection paperwork, and provides final permission to operate?"*
- **Request:** Permit application and issued permit, approved electrical plans, equipment specifications, SMUD application or interconnection record, inspection sign-off, and permission-to-operate confirmation.

The City of Sacramento's residential solar page explains that residential PV permits require contractor information, construction plans, equipment specifications, and an SMUD interconnection letter. The city's automated SolarAPP+ route is limited to eligible systems and registered licensed contractors. Battery retrofits may not fit every automated path, so the installer should confirm the proper plan-review route rather than assume a standard PV application covers the storage work.

SMUD says a building permit is required for all installations, including homeowner-installed systems. Its battery page also explains that adding storage to an existing solar system does not carry the same one-time interconnection fee as a new solar-plus-storage or battery-only installation. Confirm the current utility process for your exact project.

## 5. Compare the full cost and warranty package

- **Ask:** *"What is the total installed price after the incentive, and what costs could still be added?"*
- **Request:** Itemized quote, financing agreement, incentive eligibility confirmation, equipment warranty, installer workmanship warranty, battery throughput or cycle terms, and monitoring/service plan.

The quote should include battery equipment, inverter or gateway, electrical panel upgrades, backup subpanel, wiring, trenching or conduit, mounting, permits, utility work, commissioning, and required fire or site modifications. It should state whether the installer or homeowner is responsible for program enrollment and what happens if the system misses an incentive deadline.

Read the battery manufacturer's warranty and the installer's labor warranty separately. Ask who responds if the battery stops communicating, a backup circuit fails, or the manufacturer requires a replacement. Confirm whether labor to remove and reinstall equipment is covered.

## Warning signs

- The salesperson cannot explain the difference between battery capacity and power output.
- Savings claims do not use your actual SMUD rate, consumption, or existing solar arrangement.
- The quote does not identify backed-up circuits or explain whole-home backup limits.
- The company will not provide license numbers or identify electrical subcontractors.
- The installer says permits or SMUD approval are unnecessary without written confirmation.
- Incentive amounts are guaranteed without checking current eligibility and deadlines.
- The contract requires most payment before installation, inspection, and commissioning.
- No one is clearly responsible for utility enrollment, monitoring, warranty calls, or system handoff.

## Before you book

- Gather existing solar plans, utility bills, rate information, and permission-to-operate record.
- Verify each contractor's CSLB license and insurance.
- Get at least two or three bids based on the same backup-load design.
- Compare usable kWh, continuous kW, peak output, and backed-up circuits.
- Confirm city permits, SMUD interconnection, rate changes, and current incentive eligibility.
- Put equipment, panel work, commissioning, enrollment, and warranties in writing.
- Tie payments to installation, inspection, and completed handoff milestones.

SMUD serves a defined territory and local permitting differs across Sacramento city and county jurisdictions. Verify current requirements, rates, incentives, and permit paths for your exact address before signing.
