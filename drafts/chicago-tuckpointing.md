Chicago masonry repairs can look simple from the sidewalk. A contractor removes failing mortar, packs in new material, and the wall looks better. The trouble is that a masonry wall is a system. Mortar that is too hard, too deep, poorly matched, or installed without addressing water entry can damage the brick and turn a maintenance project into a structural repair.

Chicago's building-permit guidance treats routine masonry maintenance, including tuckpointing, as a defined work type. The city also distinguishes larger or structural work, such as major lintel replacement or rebuilding masonry wythes, from routine repair. Your first job is to find out which project you actually have.

## 1. Ask whether this is routine tuckpointing or structural work

- **Ask:** “What exactly is failing, and how do you know?”
- **Request:** A written inspection with photographs marking open joints, spalling brick, lintels, parapets, cracks, and areas of water intrusion.
- **Why it matters:** A contractor who labels every problem “tuckpointing” may be selling a surface treatment for a structural or water-management issue. A contractor who labels every crack “structural” may be selling fear.

Ask whether the scope includes only mortar-joint replacement or also brick replacement, lintel work, parapet rebuilding, flashing, coping, or interior water-control work. Those are not interchangeable line items.

## 2. Compare the mortar specification, not just the square-foot price

Your written quote should identify:

- Mortar type and intended strength
- Color and joint profile
- Rake-out depth
- Whether joints will be tested before full production
- Brick replacement method
- Cleaning method
- Cold-weather or rain protection
- Caulking, flashing, and sealant details
- Final cleanup and disposal

A quote that says “tuckpoint entire rear wall” leaves too much room for substitutions. Ask the contractor to create a small sample area and approve it before the full wall is completed. The finished color, tooling, and brick appearance should be judged in person, not from a glossy brochure.

## 3. Verify permits and building responsibility

Chicago's Department of Buildings says routine masonry maintenance and repair, including tuckpointing, may fit the Express Permit Program within defined limits. Work involving structural elements or larger repairs may require drawings prepared by a licensed architect or structural engineer. Ask:

- Is a permit required for this exact scope?
- Who will pull it?
- What permit number will appear on the contract?
- Are sidewalk, scaffolding, alley, or public-way permissions needed?
- Will the contractor protect neighboring property and pedestrians?

Do not accept “no permit needed” as a complete answer. Ask the city or your building department directly, especially for a multi-unit building, historic district, parapet, chimney, or lintel project.

## 4. Protect the building from the wrong repair

Request proof that the contractor has experience with:

- Chicago brick and older masonry assemblies
- Historic or soft brick
- Freeze-thaw conditions
- Parapets, chimneys, and cornices
- Water entry at window heads and sills
- Multi-unit access and tenant coordination

Ask for two recent references where the contractor repaired a similar wall and the owner can describe how it performed after a full winter. A five-star review from a driveway project does not prove competence on a three-story masonry building.

## Warning signs

- The contractor will not name the mortar or provide a sample panel.
- The estimate includes “repair as needed” with no unit prices or limits.
- The company wants to grind every joint without discussing brick condition.
- The proposal ignores lintels, parapets, flashing, or water entry.
- The contractor cannot explain permit responsibility.
- The warranty covers only “materials” and excludes workmanship or leaks.
- A salesperson pressures you to sign before you can inspect references.

## Before you book

- Get a photo-marked scope and a sample mortar panel.
- Compare equal scopes with the same joint depth, materials, access, and cleanup.
- Verify licenses, insurance, and permit responsibility.
- Ask how the contractor will protect windows, roofs, landscaping, sidewalks, and occupants.
- Hold final payment until the work is inspected from the ground and close up.
- Save the permit, photos, contract, warranty, and maintenance recommendations.
