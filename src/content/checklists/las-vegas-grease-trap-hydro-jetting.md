Las Vegas concentrates 24/7 restaurants, casinos, and food-service operations that push heavy fats, oils, and grease (FOG) into drainage systems. Standard grease-trap pumpers often skim liquid waste while hardened grease remains on interior pipe walls. When inspectors flag slow drains or a backup threatens a peak service period, operators need hydro-jetting—not another skim. Use these questions before you book commercial line scouring.

## Questions to ask before you book

* **Do you hydro-jet the outlet lines and interceptor walls, or only pump the trap?** Ask whether the service includes high-pressure water jetting to strip hardened FOG from internal drain walls, not just liquid and sludge removal from the tank.
* **How often should we schedule jetting in addition to regular pumping?** Many high-volume kitchens combine pumping with jetting on a set interval. Ask for a recommendation based on your seating, menu (especially fryers and charbroilers), and observed backup history—not a generic quarterly card.
* **Will you inspect outlet sanitary tees, baffles, and accessible cleanouts?** Confirm they check for calcified grease that can cause sudden kitchen backups even when the trap itself was recently pumped.
* **Do you use or recommend chemical degreasers or additives?** Many FOG programs restrict products that emulsify grease so it passes downstream. Confirm against current Clark County / local wastewater guidance before agreeing to any additive program.
* **Are you equipped and permitted for commercial grease waste lines in the Las Vegas area?** Ask about licensing, disposal destinations, and whether they regularly service restaurant and casino interceptors—not only residential septic work.
* **What documentation will I receive?** Keep dated service logs of pumping and jetting for health and environmental inspections. Ask for manifests where required and a written note of sections jetted.
* **What is the price and emergency availability?** Get the scope in writing, including after-hours or emergency rates if drains are already slow or backing up before a weekend rush.

## Preventing kitchen floods with commercial hydro-jetting in Las Vegas

Las Vegas food-service volume is relentless. Interceptors hold FOG back, but pumping alone often leaves a hardened layer on outlet pipe walls. That layer narrows effective diameter until a complete blockage hits during peak hours—exactly when a casino café or Strip restaurant can least afford a shutdown.

Standard plumbing companies frequently avoid greasy commercial waste lines because of the mess, disposal rules, and specialized equipment required. When slow drains or an inspector’s notice appear, managers need heavy-duty intervention rather than another routine pump-out. Specialized commercial hydro-jetting crews use high-pressure water to scour lines toward bare pipe, restore capacity, and support compliance with municipal FOG programs.

Local context that matters: Clark County and City of Las Vegas wastewater and pretreatment programs expect food-service facilities to maintain interceptors and traps, keep service records, and prevent FOG from entering the collection system. Confirm current interceptor sizing, pumping frequency, and any jetting or additive rules with the authority that serves your address. Pair scheduled pumping with periodic jetting of outlet lines, keep clear logs on site, and treat emergency jetting as a backstop—not the primary maintenance plan.

## What good FOG documentation looks like

A useful service package usually includes: date and time of service; interceptor location and size if known; volumes pumped; confirmation that outlet lines or specified runs were jetted; notes on tees, baffles, and any defects found; disposal or manifest information where required; and a recommended next service date. Store logs where managers and inspectors can find them. A pattern of regular pump-plus-jet records is stronger evidence of good-faith maintenance than a single emergency ticket after a backup.

If you manage multiple outlets (casino food courts, multi-concept buildings), ask whether the contractor can standardize reporting across locations and flag traps that need more frequent jetting based on actual grease load rather than a single calendar for every unit.
