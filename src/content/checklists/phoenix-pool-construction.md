Building a backyard pool in the Phoenix area is a major construction project, not a simple equipment purchase. The price can depend on excavation access, soil and rock, pool shape, structural design, plumbing runs, electrical bonding, decking, drainage, fencing, heaters, automation, and landscaping restoration. A low headline price may leave important work outside the contract.

There is a concrete consumer-risk signal. On June 25, 2026, the Arizona Registrar of Contractors announced that it revoked a Scottsdale pool builder's dual swimming-pool contractor license after receiving 85 complaints from homeowners and subcontractors between September 9, 2025 and June 25, 2026. The agency said the company failed to answer a citation. This is an enforcement action involving one company, not a claim that 85 different pool builders or the industry as a whole have the same problem. It does show why customers should verify a license, check complaint history, and protect progress payments instead of relying on a showroom, referral, or polished sales presentation.

Phoenix has a defined permit and inspection path. The City requires a residential pool permit and publishes plan-submittal guidance, pool-barrier requirements, and inspection information. Its pool-barrier document describes inspections before gunite and before plaster, along with barrier and electrical-bonding requirements. Your builder should explain how the project will satisfy current code and who is responsible for each inspection.

## 1. Verify the contractor and exact license classification

- **Ask:** *"What is the legal name on the contract, your Arizona Registrar of Contractors license number, and the classification covering this exact pool project?"*
- **Request:** Current license printout, general liability insurance, workers' compensation coverage, and names and license numbers of electrical, plumbing, gas, and other subcontractors.
- **Check:** Search the Arizona Registrar of Contractors database for active status, classification, disciplinary history, and complaint information.

Arizona requires contractors performing covered construction and repair work to be licensed before bidding. The ROC lists swimming-pool classifications, including A-9 and B-5 scopes. Do not accept a generic statement that the company is "licensed." Confirm that the named license holder and classification match the work being sold. If the builder uses subcontractors, ask who is contractually responsible for their work and whether each trade is properly licensed.

## 2. Make the plans and price describe the same pool

- **Ask:** *"Can I have the complete construction plans and a line-item proposal before I sign?"*
- **Request:** Site plan, pool dimensions and depth profile, structural details, equipment schedule, plumbing layout, electrical plan, finish selections, decking dimensions, and written exclusions.

The proposal should identify excavation, haul-off, steel, plumbing, pressure testing, gunite or shotcrete, tile, coping, interior finish, startup, decking, fencing or barrier work, equipment pad, pump, filter, sanitation, lighting, automation, and cleanup. State whether rock excavation, soil export, retaining walls, drainage changes, utility relocation, and landscaping are included or priced separately.

A pool drawing is not a complete quote if it leaves out the deck, access restoration, equipment, or safety barrier. Compare bids using the same dimensions, finish, equipment capacity, and included work. Ask each builder to identify allowances and explain what happens if selected tile, decking, or equipment costs more than the allowance.

## 3. Confirm Phoenix permits, inspections, and barriers

- **Ask:** *"Who applies for the City of Phoenix pool permit, posts approved plans, schedules inspections, and obtains final approval?"*
- **Request:** Permit application, approved plans, permit number, inspection schedule, and written barrier-compliance plan.

Phoenix publishes a residential pool/spa permit process and requires inspections for permitted work. Its pool-barrier guidance describes inspections before gunite and before plaster, and explains acceptable barrier approaches. Details depend on the property's layout and adopted code. Do not assume an existing block wall, sliding door, or pool cover automatically satisfies current requirements.

Ask who handles separate permits for gas lines, electrical work, retaining structures, shade structures, or other additions. A pool permit may not cover every feature in a backyard package. Verify requirements with Phoenix or the city where the property sits. Scottsdale, Mesa, Glendale, and unincorporated Maricopa County may follow different permitting authorities.

## 4. Protect deposits and progress payments

- **Ask:** *"What measurable milestone does each payment correspond to, and what happens if work stops or the schedule slips?"*
- **Request:** Signed contract, payment schedule, change-order form, subcontractor list, lien waivers as appropriate, and written cancellation terms.

Avoid paying a large portion before permits, excavation, or materials are in place. Tie payments to completed stages that can be verified. Payments should go to the contracting business named on the license and contract. If the builder asks you to pay a subcontractor or supplier directly, document why and obtain a receipt and appropriate release.

The contract should state a realistic start window, estimated completion range, weather and supply-delay provisions, cleanup obligations, warranty coverage, and dispute process. Do not sign a blank completion certificate or final payment authorization before the punch list is complete.

## 5. Ask who commissions and supports the equipment

- **Ask:** *"Who starts the pool, balances the water, programs the controls, and shows me how to operate every component?"*
- **Request:** Equipment manuals, model and serial numbers, manufacturer warranties, builder workmanship warranty, startup record, water-chemistry instructions, and service contact details.

Confirm that lights, pumps, filters, heaters, automation, drains, and safety equipment operate as promised. Ask which company handles a leak, failed pump, finish defect, or warranty claim after the builder's crew leaves.

## Warning signs

- The company will not provide its ROC number or the classification does not match pool construction.
- A salesperson says permits or inspections are unnecessary without written confirmation.
- The quote is a lump sum with no equipment schedule, dimensions, or exclusions.
- A large deposit is demanded before permits and construction documents are ready.
- Barrier, electrical bonding, gas, or subcontractor responsibilities are vague.
- The company asks you to sign a completion form before startup and punch-list work are finished.

## Before you book

- Verify the legal business name, active ROC license, and correct pool classification.
- Review complaint and disciplinary records, not just online reviews.
- Obtain comparable bids with matching plans and equipment.
- Confirm permits, barrier details, inspections, and separate trade permits.
- Put excavation, rock, decking, landscaping, startup, and cleanup in the scope.
- Tie payments to completed milestones and document releases.
- Keep plans, permits, inspection results, warranties, and equipment manuals.

A pool is a long-term improvement and a serious safety responsibility. Verify current requirements with the building department for your exact address before signing, especially outside Phoenix city limits.
