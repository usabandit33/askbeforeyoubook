## Questions to ask before you book

* **Do you clean the entire exhaust system to bare metal, or only the visible hood and filters?** NFPA 96 expects grease removal from the hood canopy, filters, plenum, accessible ductwork, and exhaust fan—not a filter wash alone. Ask what they open and scrape.
* **How do you set the cleaning frequency for our cooking volume?** NFPA 96 intervals scale with cooking type: solid-fuel and high-volume (charbroil, wok, 24-hour) need more frequent service than moderate-volume full-service lines. Ask for a written recommendation tied to your menu and hours.
* **Will we receive a service tag, written report, and before/after photos?** Charlotte Fire Department and insurance carriers often expect documentation. Confirm what you keep on site after each visit.
* **Do you handle rooftop fan cleaning and grease containment boxes?** Exhaust fans and roof-side grease cups are part of the system; incomplete fan cleaning is a common inspection finding.
* **Are access panels adequate, or will you identify where new openings are needed?** Long duct runs cannot be cleaned properly without access. Ask whether they will note missing access points in the report.
* **Are you experienced with Charlotte / Mecklenburg fire authority expectations?** City of Charlotte and unincorporated Mecklenburg can differ; confirm they know which jurisdiction applies to your address.
* **What is the price, after-hours availability, and what is excluded?** Get scope in writing—hood only vs. full system, emergency rates if an inspection is already scheduled, and whether suppression system links or nozzles are outside the cleaning scope.

## Staying inspection-ready with commercial exhaust cleaning in Charlotte

Charlotte’s food scene generates heavy daily grease loads. Grease that coats hood interiors, duct walls, and fan blades is highly flammable. NFPA 96 (Standard for Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations) sets the national baseline for how thoroughly and how often commercial exhaust systems must be cleaned. Local fire authorities adopt and enforce those requirements; failing an accumulation inspection can lead to notices, forced shutdowns, or insurance complications.

Many general sanitation companies focus on floors and surfaces and lack the scaffolding, roof access, and scraping process needed for full-system degreasing. Restaurant operators then scramble for certified crews when an inspector is already on the calendar. Specialized commercial hood and exhaust cleaners close that gap by cleaning to bare metal where required, documenting the work, and scheduling intervals that match actual cooking volume rather than a generic calendar.

Local context that matters: for kitchens inside Charlotte city limits, start with the Charlotte Fire Department; unincorporated Mecklenburg is handled through the county fire marshal. Confirm the current inspection and documentation expectations for your specific address. Keep dated tags and reports on site. Suppression system inspection, testing, and maintenance is a separate schedule—do not assume a hood cleaning resets those due dates.
