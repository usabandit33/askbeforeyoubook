Whether you are building a new custom home in Apex, adding a pool in Cary, renovating an older home in North Raleigh, or repairing a failing drainfield in Wake Forest, navigating septic regulations requires dealing with Wake County Environmental Services (WCES). In North Carolina, modifying or installing an onsite wastewater system is governed by strict state rules (15A NCAC 18E).

A frequent source of costly project delays and contract disputes is confusing an **Improvement Permit (IP)** with a **Construction Authorization (CA)**. Paying a contractor or excavator to begin digging before holding a valid Construction Authorization is illegal and can lead to stop-work orders and non-refundable fees.

Use this Wake County checklist to protect your property and ensure your septic project follows the legal approval pipeline.

## 1. Understand the difference between an Improvement Permit and a Construction Authorization

Wake County enforces a two-stage permitting process before a single shovel enters the ground:

- **Improvement Permit (IP):** An IP evaluates the land and soil suitability. An Environmental Health Specialist conducts soil auger borings to assess texture, structure, seasonal high water tables (soil mottling/redoximorphic features), and depth to bedrock. If suitable, the IP designates the general absorption area and the legally mandated **100% repair area**. An IP does *not* authorize construction; you cannot pull building permits with an IP alone.
- **Construction Authorization (CA):** The CA is the actual building permit for the septic system. It specifies the exact system type (conventional gravity gravel trenches, chamber systems, low-pressure pipe / LPP, or advanced pre-treatment drip irrigation), daily design flow, trench depth, and precise layout geometry based on the final house footprint and driveway location.
- **Ask:** *"Do we currently hold a signed Construction Authorization specifying the exact footprint of my proposed home or addition, or only an Improvement Permit?"*

## 2. Check existing permits on Wake County iMAPS before planning additions

If you are remodeling a home or adding bedrooms (e.g., finishing an attic or basement in Raleigh), your septic system must be sized for the total potential occupancy.

- **Ask:** *"What does the original Wake County Operations Permit list as the approved bedroom capacity?"*
- **The rule in NC:** Under North Carolina law, septic capacity is calculated strictly by the number of bedrooms (typically 120 gallons per day per bedroom), not bathrooms. If your home has a 3-bedroom permit, you cannot legally connect a 4th bedroom without submitting an application to Wake County for an expansion or system upgrade.
- You can look up historical septic permits and original site sketch maps using the Wake County Permit Portal or the Wake County iMAPS GIS database.

## 3. Protect the designated 100% repair area

North Carolina regulations require almost all residential lots to have a designated, undisturbed reserve area equal in size to the original drainfield.

- **Ask:** *"Where is the legally designated septic repair area located on the plat, and will your construction equipment or driveway encroach on it?"*
- **Warning:** If a contractor parks heavy equipment, drives dump trucks, or excavates over the designated repair area, the soil will become compacted and unusable. If a site loses its repair area due to soil compaction, you may be forced to install an expensive engineered mechanical pre-treatment system costing $25,000 to $40,000.

## 4. Verify NCOWCIC installer certification level

Under North Carolina law, septic installers must be certified through the North Carolina Onsite Wastewater Contractors and Inspectors Certification Board (NCOWCIC).
- Installers are certified by level (Level I through Level IV). Complex engineered systems—such as low-pressure pipe (LPP) or aerobic treatment units (ATUs)—require a Level III or Level IV certified contractor.
- Confirm your installer holds the appropriate certification level for your specific system design before signing a contract.

## 5. Never backfill trenches until the county inspector issues the Operation Permit

- The contractor must schedule an open-trench inspection with Wake County Environmental Services before covering the pipe and gravel/chambers.
- The inspector verifies pipe grade, aggregate depth, and distribution box leveling. Only after passing this inspection and receiving the official **Operation Permit (OP)** can the building department issue the Certificate of Occupancy (CO) for the home.
