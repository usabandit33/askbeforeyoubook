From coastal communities like Pacific Beach and La Jolla to hot inland valleys across Escondido, Poway, and El Cajon, homes across San Diego County face distinct thermal challenges. In summer, direct coastal sun turns unventilated attics into 130°F to 145°F heat ovens, radiating heat down through the ceiling drywall into living rooms and bedrooms.

When homeowners look to lower their electric bills, contractors often offer two drastically different bids: a cheap quote to "blow new fiberglass over the existing layer," versus a comprehensive proposal to "pull" (vacuum extract) all old material, sanitize, air-seal the attic deck, and blow in fresh insulation to modern California Title 24 standards.

Use this checklist to decide between adding insulation over your current layer or performing a complete pull-and-seal extraction.

## 1. Know when old insulation MUST be pulled (extracted)

Blowing new insulation over contaminated or damaged material traps odors, toxins, and moisture inside your home.

- **Rodent contamination:** San Diego is home to roof rats (*Rattus rattus*) that nest in attic insulation. If your attic has rat droppings, urine trails, or tunnel runs, blowing new insulation directly over the mess traps biohazard pheromones that attract future rodents and pollute your indoor air.
- **Previous roof leaks or mold:** Wet, compressed fiberglass loses its R-value permanently and becomes a breeding ground for mold spores.
- **Extreme compression:** If existing fiberglass or rockwool is 30+ years old and compressed into a dirty 2-inch mat full of construction dust, it cannot insulate effectively.
- **Ask:** *"Did your inspection uncover rodent droppings, mold, or compressed batts, and do you recommend a complete vacuum extraction with HEPA truck containment?"*

## 2. Air sealing is far more important than insulation alone

The biggest mistake homeowners make is buying insulation without sealing air leaks first.

- **The science:** Insulation works like a wool sweater: it slows down conductive heat transfer, but if wind blows through the knit, you stay cold. Air sealing is the windbreaker.
- **Ask:** *"Does your quote include moving existing insulation aside to air-seal all ceiling penetrations with expanding fire-rated polyurethane foam?"*
- **What must be sealed:** Top plates of interior wall framing, wire penetrations, recessed can lights, plumbing vent stacks, bath exhaust fan housings, and attic access drop-down stairs. Sealing these gaps prevents conditioned indoor air from leaking into the attic.

## 3. Verify target R-value under California Title 24 standards

California’s Building Energy Efficiency Standards (Title 24, Part 6) set specific thermal performance metrics for residential attics.

- **Ask:** *"What final R-value and installed thickness will you achieve, and will you install insulation depth rulers and a certified certificate at the attic hatch?"*
- **The target:** Across San Diego (Climate Zones 7 and 10), the recommended ceiling insulation standard is **R-38 to R-49** (approximately 11 to 14 inches of loose-fill cellulose or 12 to 16 inches of virgin fiberglass).
- Under state rules, the contractor must staple depth markers throughout the attic and post a signed certificate stating the number of bags installed and manufacturer specs.

## 4. Install soffit vent baffles to protect attic airflow

Insulation must never block your roof’s natural airflow.

- **Ask:** *"Will you install rafter vent baffles (AccuVent or Durovent foam chutes) at every soffit intake to keep blown insulation from blocking ventilation?"*
- **Why it matters:** In coastal Southern California, blocked soffit vents trap moist marine air in the attic, leading to condensation, wood rot, and premature degradation of roof asphalt shingles. Baffles ensure continuous airflow from eaves up to ridge vents.

## 5. Check for SDG&E and California Energy Upgrade rebates

San Diego Gas & Electric (SDG&E) and state clean-energy initiatives periodically offer financial incentives or tax credits for verified whole-home air sealing and insulation upgrades that improve HERS ratings.
- Ask your contractor if they are an approved participating contractor with local energy efficiency programs and can provide the necessary invoices for federal Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credits (Section 25C).
