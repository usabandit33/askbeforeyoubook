Melbourne sits along the Indian River Lagoon with thousands of private docks and boat lifts exposed to salt air, humidity, and brackish water. When a lift motor fails, a shore-power connection shorts, or dock wiring corrodes, the boat is stranded and the dock becomes a shock hazard. Hauling the vessel to a busy yard or waiting weeks for a general electrician who refuses waterfront work is not a practical option for most owners.

Florida treats dock and boat-lift electrical work as licensed electrical work. New circuits, most significant repairs, and equipment that serves boat hoists generally require permits and inspections. The National Electrical Code (NEC) Article 555 and related Florida Building Code provisions govern marina and docking-facility wiring, including GFCI protection that is critical for preventing electric-shock drowning.

## 1. Confirm the exact problem before inviting bids

A stuck boat lift can be a failed motor, a bad contactor or drum switch, corroded connections, a tripped or undersized breaker, voltage drop on a long dock run, or a control issue. Dock outlets that keep tripping may point to ground faults, improper bonding, or deteriorated marine-rated cable.

**Ask:** "Can you diagnose whether this is a motor, control, wiring, GFCI, or power-supply problem before quoting a full replacement?"

Request a written description of the findings and the specific components that will be replaced or repaired. Do not accept a vague "we'll get it running" estimate that leaves the root cause undefined.

## 2. Verify the contractor holds the right Florida electrical license

Electrical work on docks and boat lifts in Melbourne and Brevard County must be performed by a properly licensed electrical contractor or electrician working under that license. Florida's Construction Industry Licensing Board issues electrical contractor licenses; local building departments enforce permits and inspections. Marine experience matters because ordinary residential wiring practices fail quickly in a salt-air, wet environment.

**Ask:** "What is the exact legal business name and Florida electrical contractor license number that will appear on the permit and my contract?"

Check the license status yourself through the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) license search. Ask whether the person who will actually work on the dock is the qualifier or an employee operating under that license. Confirm general liability and workers' compensation coverage that applies to waterfront work.

A Florida marine contractor license covers docks, seawalls, and boat-lift structures, but the electrical circuit feeding the lift is still electrical work and typically requires a licensed electrician. Clarify who is responsible for each portion of the job.

## 3. Demand GFCI protection and marine-rated materials

NEC requirements for docking facilities emphasize ground-fault protection. Boat-hoist outlets at dwelling-unit docks generally require GFCI protection. Wet-location wiring, corrosion-resistant enclosures, proper bonding, and weatherproof connections are not optional in the Indian River environment.

**Ask:** "Will every circuit serving the lift and dock receptacles have appropriate GFCI or ground-fault protection, and will you use marine-rated wire, conduit, and enclosures?"

Ask the contractor to explain how the installation will address voltage drop on long dock runs and how the system will be bonded. Do not accept residential indoor-grade cable or enclosures on a dock exposed to salt spray.

## 4. Clarify permits, inspections, and who pulls them

New electrical circuits and most significant dock or boat-lift electrical work require permits from the applicable building department (City of Melbourne or Brevard County, depending on the address). The contractor should identify the correct jurisdiction and pull the electrical permit.

**Ask:** "Is the electrical permit included in your price, which building department will issue it, and will you schedule the inspection?"

Confirm that the scope on the permit matches the work being performed. Keep a copy of the issued permit and the final inspection result with your property records.

## 5. Compare complete written scopes, not hourly rates alone

A useful proposal should identify:

- Diagnostic findings and root cause.
- Exact components to be replaced (motor, contactor, cable, enclosure, GFCI device, etc.).
- Whether the circuit is being upgraded, relocated, or merely repaired.
- Marine-rated materials and corrosion-protection steps.
- Permit and inspection responsibility.
- Testing and verification after the work (operation under load, GFCI test, visual inspection of connections).
- Warranty on parts and labor.

**Ask:** "What is excluded from this price, and what happens if the inspection or further diagnosis reveals additional corrosion or code issues?"

Compare at least two detailed proposals. The lowest hourly rate can become the most expensive job if the contractor uses the wrong materials or leaves the underlying fault unaddressed.

## 6. Protect yourself on payment and documentation

Use a written contract that names the licensed contractor, describes the work, states the price or clear unit pricing, and identifies payment milestones. Avoid large upfront payments before diagnosis and materials are confirmed. Request before-and-after photos of critical connections and a written summary of what was done.

Keep the invoice, permit, inspection record, and any manufacturer literature for the new components. If the lift or dock is later sold or insured, documentation of code-compliant electrical work is valuable.

## Warning signs

- The contractor cannot produce a current Florida electrical contractor license number.
- The quote assumes indoor residential wire or non-GFCI protection is acceptable on a dock.
- The company refuses to pull a permit for new circuit work or significant alterations.
- The estimate is a single line item with no diagnosis or parts list.
- The technician is unfamiliar with NEC Article 555 or electric-shock drowning risks.
- Payment is demanded in full before the work is inspected or tested under load.
- The contractor dismisses corrosion or bonding issues as "normal for Florida."

## Before you book

Confirm the diagnosis in writing, verify the Florida electrical license and insurance, require marine-rated materials and proper GFCI protection, and make the permit and final inspection part of the contract. Test the completed work under load and keep the documentation. Verify current permit requirements with the City of Melbourne Building Department or Brevard County Building Department for your exact address before work begins.
