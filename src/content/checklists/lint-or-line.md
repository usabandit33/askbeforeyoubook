A clothes dryer that takes two or three cycles to dry a single load of towels is not just an energy drain—it is a critical household fire hazard. According to the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA), clothes dryers account for thousands of residential structure fires each year, with failure to clean lint being the leading contributing factor.

In the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex, modern multi-story homes and master-planned subdivisions often place laundry rooms in interior rooms or second-floor hallways. This requires long, concealed exhaust ducts snaking 20 to 35 feet through unconditioned attics to exterior roof jacks.

Before hiring a dryer vent cleaning or repair service in Dallas, use this checklist to determine whether you need a routine lint sweep, a hazardous flexible foil replacement, or a code-compliant rigid metal reroute.

## 1. Eliminate combustible foil and vinyl accordion ducting

Flexible plastic (white vinyl) and thin wire-ribbed foil accordion ducts are major fire hazards and violate modern building codes.

- **Ask:** *"What material connects the back of the dryer to the wall, and what material runs through the walls/attic?"*
- **Code rules (IRC Section M1502):** The International Residential Code requires dryer exhaust ducts to be constructed of smooth-walled rigid metal (minimum 0.016-inch thick, equivalent to 28-gauge galvanized steel or aluminum) with a 4-inch nominal diameter. Flexible semi-rigid transition ducts are permitted *only* between the dryer and the wall connection (maximum 8 feet), and must be UL 2158A listed.
- **Why it matters:** Ridged accordion ducts trap lint in every corrugated groove. When clothes dryers overheat, combustible vinyl or thin foil melts instantly, allowing flames to shoot directly into the wall cavity.

## 2. Check the total equivalent length and elbow reductions

Long duct runs with multiple bends create massive backpressure that chokes off airflow.

- **Ask:** *"What is the total equivalent length of the duct run, and does it exceed the 35-foot IRC maximum without a booster fan?"*
- **Calculating length:** Under building code, the maximum allowed length of a clothes dryer exhaust duct is 35 feet from the dryer to the wall or roof termination. This maximum must be reduced by 5 feet for every 90-degree elbow and 2.5 feet for every 45-degree elbow. A duct running 20 feet with three 90-degree bends has an equivalent length of 35 feet (20 + 15 = 35), right at the legal limit.

## 3. Verify the cleaning method: rotary brush & high-powered vacuum

Blowing air through a line with a leaf blower or running a simple hand snake will not remove baked-on, moist lint cakes.

- **Ask:** *"Do you use a motorized rotary brush spinning system paired with a commercial HEPA negative-air vacuum, and do you measure backpressure before and after?"*
- **Verification:** A professional technician measures airflow velocity at the exterior vent hood using an anemometer or backpressure gauge. Airflow should increase dramatically after a complete cleaning.

## 4. Inspect exterior roof cap and wall flappers

In North Texas, exterior vent terminations face intense sun and wind-driven rain.
- **Ask:** *"Will your service include inspecting the exterior wall damper or roof cap to ensure the flapper opens freely?"*
- **Crucial code rule:** Exterior dryer vent hoods must have a backdraft damper, but **must never have a protective wire mesh or screen**. Wire screens quickly accumulate lint, forming an impenetrable felt-like plug that traps lint and carbon monoxide inside the home.

## 5. Get transparent pricing for cleaning vs. repairs

- Ask for the base price for a 1-story ground-floor run versus a 2-story attic/roof run.
- If the technician claims the duct needs to be replaced or rerouted, require them to show photographic evidence of disconnected joints, crushed transition hoses, or standing water condensation in the attic before approving repairs.
