From historic craftsman bungalows in Inman Park to newly constructed multi-story townhomes in Midtown and West Midtown, dryer vent configurations across Atlanta present distinct fire and moisture hazards. In Georgia’s humid climate, warm, moist exhaust air travelling through an uninsulated duct in a damp crawl space or ventilated attic condenses into liquid water, turning dry lint into thick, cement-like sludge that clogs pipes and creates severe fire hazards.

Homeowners are often quoted expensive duct replacements when a thorough mechanical sweeping would restore full airflow. Conversely, some cleaning companies fail to warn homeowners about illegal materials or dangerous screws inside their walls.

Before scheduling a dryer vent technician in Atlanta, use this checklist to clarify cleaning methods, code compliance, and repair scope.

## 1. Verify that no screws penetrate the duct interior

A common and hazardous installation error made by amateur handymen is fastening dryer vent sections together with standard sheet metal screws.

- **Ask:** *"Are the joints in my dryer duct fastened with foil tape or clamps, and are there any sheet metal screws piercing the inside of the pipe?"*
- **Why it matters:** The International Residential Code (IRC Section M1502.4.2) explicitly forbids screws or any fasteners that protrude more than 1/8 inch into the inside of a dryer exhaust pipe. Screws act like fishhooks, snagging wet lint fibres with every load until a dense, flammable plug forms directly inside your ceiling or wall framing.
- **Approved joints:** All rigid metal joints must be installed in the direction of airflow and sealed using heat-resistant aluminum foil tape (rated UL 181A-P or 181B-FX) or exterior drawband clamps—never standard grey cloth duct tape, which dries out and fails within two years.

## 2. Insist on a complete rotary brush sweep with vacuum containment

Lint clings electrostatically to the inner metal walls of exhaust ducts.

- **Ask:** *"Do you clean the duct using a motorized spinning rotary brush and commercial HEPA vacuum, or do you only blow air through with a leaf blower?"*
- **Why it matters:** Simply blowing compressed air or running a blower from the outside can push a wet clog backwards into the dryer’s internal blower wheel or pack lint tightly into the nearest 90-degree elbow. A rotary brush scrubs the metal walls clean and pulls lint out into a filtered containment unit.

## 3. Check for condensation in Atlanta crawl spaces and attics

In Georgia, dryer ducts often travel through unconditioned crawl spaces or hot summer attics before exiting through the exterior foundation or roof.

- **Ask:** *"Does the duct pass through unconditioned attic or crawl space areas, and is it insulated to prevent condensation?"*
- When 130°F humid exhaust air hits a cold metal pipe in a Georgia crawl space during winter, water instantly condenses on the pipe floor. This standing water traps lint, corrodes galvanized steel, and can leak into ceilings. Insulating the duct with R-6 to R-8 foil-faced duct wrap prevents condensation.

## 4. Upgrade transition ducts to UL 2158A semi-rigid metal

The transition duct is the flexible hose connecting the back of your dryer to the wall collar.
- Throw away white ribbed plastic or paper-thin foil hoses. They are combustible and easily crushed when the dryer is pushed back against the wall, cutting off 70% of airflow.
- Install a heavy-duty, semi-rigid aluminum transition duct certified to UL 2158A standards.

## 5. Verify the exterior termination and pest protection

Atlanta’s tree canopy means squirrels, chipmunks, and birds frequently nest in open vent hoods.
- Ensure the exterior wall or roof hood has a working gravity backdraft damper that snaps shut when the dryer turns off.
- Verify that **no wire mesh or screening** has been placed over the opening, as lint will quickly plug the screen and trap poisonous carbon monoxide inside gas-dryer homes.
