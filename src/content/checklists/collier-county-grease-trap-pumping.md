Restaurants and other food-service businesses in Naples and unincorporated Collier County need a dependable grease-management routine. A missed pump-out can become more than an unpleasant kitchen problem: fats, oils, and grease can contribute to sewer blockages, backups, and compliance trouble. Collier County's food-service guidance says accumulated grease and food waste must be pumped at least every 90 days, while also telling businesses not to use chemicals, enzymes, emulsifiers, live bacteria, or other grease-cutting additives without county approval. The important booking question is therefore not simply "How much to pump it?" It is "Will this hauler meet the local rules and give me records I can produce during an inspection?"

## 1. Does the company understand which authority regulates my address?

- **Ask:** *"Is my kitchen connected to Collier County's wastewater system, the City of Naples system, or another utility, and do you know which rules apply?"*
- **Red flag:** A hauler who gives you a generic schedule without asking where the business is located or where its wastewater goes.

Service rules can depend on the utility serving the property. Before booking, identify the wastewater provider and ask the provider or county which grease-management requirements apply to your facility. Collier County's public guidance is a useful starting point for businesses in its service area, but do not assume it governs every address inside the broader Naples area. If your restaurant is inside a municipality or served by a different utility, ask for that authority's current requirements in writing.

## 2. Will the hauler remove the waste completely and document the job?

- **Ask:** *"Will you remove the accumulated grease, wastewater, and solids, and provide a dated service record showing what was removed and where it went?"*
- **Red flag:** A service that pumps only part of the contents, returns wastewater to the interceptor, or leaves without a disposal record.

Ask what the quoted pump-out includes: opening the unit, removing floating grease and settled solids, cleaning accessible surfaces and components, checking for obvious damage, and closing the unit securely. Confirm how the waste is transported and disposed of. Florida law addresses grease-waste handling, and the county's guidance emphasizes proper maintenance and pumping. Keep the service ticket with the date, facility, unit identification, quantity or description of waste, and hauler details. A verbal assurance is not a useful compliance record when an inspector asks for proof.

## 3. What pumping schedule applies to this facility?

- **Ask:** *"What is the required minimum interval for my unit, and what signs or measurements would require more frequent service?"*
- **Red flag:** A promise that a small dose of treatment product means you can skip scheduled pumping.

Collier County says accumulated grease and other food waste must be pumped at least every 90 days. That is a minimum, not a guarantee that every kitchen can safely wait 90 days. Menu, cooking volume, operating hours, sink practices, and interceptor capacity all affect how quickly grease accumulates. Ask the hauler to explain how it will help you track the schedule and what condition would justify an earlier visit. Do not let a vendor substitute a product sale for a required pump-out.

## 4. Are additives allowed at my location?

- **Ask:** *"Does my wastewater authority permit this specific enzyme or biological additive, and can you show me written approval?"*
- **Red flag:** A sales pitch claiming that enzymes dissolve grease and eliminate the need for pumping.

This point deserves special attention. Collier County tells food-service establishments not to use chemicals, enzymes, emulsifiers, live bacteria, or other grease cutters or additives without county approval. The City of Tampa separately warns that additives can move grease downstream and contribute to blockages. Rules vary by authority, so never assume a product is allowed just because it is sold commercially. Get written approval for the specific product and application before using it. Even if an additive is approved, it does not replace required pumping, inspection, or recordkeeping.

## 5. Is the estimate complete, and will the hauler help prevent repeat problems?

- **Ask:** *"Does the price include disposal, access, after-hours service, and a written report, and will you point out maintenance issues?"*
- **Red flag:** A low advertised price that excludes disposal, access charges, or required documentation.

Tell the company whether the unit is under a sink, in a floor space, or in an exterior vault. Explain whether access is blocked by equipment, vehicles, or landscaping. Ask about emergency or after-hours rates before you need them. During service, ask the technician to note damaged baffles, missing covers, leaks, odors, or unusual solids. Also ask for practical kitchen controls: scrape plates into trash, use sink strainers, and keep cooking oil out of drains. These steps reduce the amount of grease entering the system, but they do not eliminate scheduled maintenance.

## Before you book

Verify the wastewater authority for the address, confirm the applicable pumping interval, use a qualified hauler, insist on complete removal and a written service record, and get written approval before using any enzyme or biological additive. Keep pump-out records together and train staff not to pour grease or food waste into sinks. If the company promises that a treatment product will replace pumping, find another provider.
