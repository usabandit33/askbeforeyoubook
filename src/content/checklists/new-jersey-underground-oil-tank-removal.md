New Jersey homes that once used heating oil may still have an underground tank in the yard, basement, or driveway area. When a tank is discovered during a sale, refinance, or renovation, the job can quickly expand from “remove the tank” to excavation, soil testing, state reporting, remediation, and a document trail that affects the property’s value.

The first mistake is assuming that every company advertising tank work can legally perform every part of the project. New Jersey distinguishes between tank closure, subsurface evaluation, and remediation. Your contractor should explain which role it is licensed and certified to perform before you sign.

## 1. Confirm what tank you have

Ask:

- “Is this an underground heating-oil tank, and what evidence supports that conclusion?”
- “Where is the tank, how large is it, and is it still connected to fuel lines?”
- “Are you recommending a sweep, removal, or both?”
- “Could another buried tank or abandoned line be present?”

Request the inspection report, photos, sweep results, prior permits, and any earlier closure paperwork. A verbal statement that “the tank was filled years ago” is not the same as proof that the tank was properly closed and the soil was checked.

If you are buying or selling, give the report to your attorney and lender early. A tank issue discovered a week before closing is a poor time to learn that the contractor’s scope did not include soil sampling or municipal sign-off.

## 2. Verify NJDEP certification by task

New Jersey requires a certified individual working for a certified underground-storage-tank firm to close an underground heating-oil tank. A company may be certified for closure but not for subsurface evaluation or remediation.

Ask:

- “What is your NJDEP certification type: HHO CLOSURE, CLOSURE, or SUBSURFACE EVALUATION?”
- “Who will be physically present when the tank is removed or closed?”
- “If a discharge is found, who will investigate and who will manage remediation?”
- “Will an LSRP be involved, and what exactly will that professional sign?”

Request the firm’s certification record, the named certified individual, insurance information, and the written scope. NJDEP’s homeowner guidance specifically recommends getting several quotes and checking the firm in its DataMiner reports.

Do not let a salesperson use a broad phrase such as “state licensed” without identifying the exact certification. Licensing language is where many expensive misunderstandings begin.

## 3. Make the quote show the whole project

A useful estimate should separate:

- Sweep or locating work.
- Tank excavation and removal.
- Cutting, hauling, and disposal.
- Soil screening and laboratory samples.
- Municipal permits and inspections.
- Tank closure paperwork.
- Emergency reporting if a discharge is suspected.
- Additional excavation or contaminated-soil handling.
- Remediation oversight and final documentation.

Ask what happens if the tank has a hole, the soil smells like oil, or groundwater is encountered. A low removal bid that excludes laboratory testing and contaminated soil is not really a low bid. It is a partial bid wearing a bargain hat.

Get at least three written estimates when time allows. Compare scope line by line rather than comparing only the headline price.

## 4. Understand what happens after a leak

NJDEP says a suspected discharge must be reported to the DEP hotline. The next steps can include investigation, remediation, a remedial action report, and a No Further Action letter when the work is complete.

Ask:

- “Who makes the call to NJDEP if contamination is found?”
- “Who pays for emergency stabilization?”
- “Will you provide laboratory results directly to me?”
- “What document proves the site is closed?”
- “Will the final package include permits, photos, soil reports, and the NJDEP correspondence?”

Do not assume the state fund will pay for ordinary tank removal or guarantee reimbursement. Confirm current eligibility directly with NJDEP before counting on public money.

## 5. Watch for red flags

- The contractor cannot show NJDEP certification.
- The contract says “remove tank” but says nothing about soil.
- A full deposit is demanded before the tank is located.
- The company promises a clean site before testing.
- You are told an NFA letter is required for every tank, even when no leak is found.
- The contractor refuses to identify who will handle reporting and remediation.
- The quote excludes permits, disposal, or municipal close-out.
- The company pressures you to sign before your attorney or lender reviews the scope.

## Before you book

Get the tank location and report in writing, verify the contractor’s certification in NJDEP’s directory, compare several itemized bids, and ask your municipality what permits and inspections apply. Keep every photo, permit, lab result, invoice, and closure document.

Verify current Newark, county, and New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection requirements before signing. Rules, forms, and program status can change, and a tank problem is too expensive to solve by guesswork.
