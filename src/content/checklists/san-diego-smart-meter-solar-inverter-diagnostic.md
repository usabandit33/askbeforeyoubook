Use these questions before you book a diagnostic audit so you know what “production problem” actually means on your roof and on your SDG&E bill.

## Questions to ask before you book

* **Will you compare inverter/monitoring production against the SDG&E smart meter export data?** A gap between the app (Enphase, SolarEdge, Tesla, SMA) and utility export readings can point to CT polarity issues, failed production meters, or under-reporting inverters. Ask how they reconcile the two and whether they will pull recent export numbers from your online utility account with you present.
* **Do you diagnose microinverter or string inverter communication failures on site?** Confirm they carry tools for Enphase Envoy/gateway, SolarEdge, and common string inverters—and that they will photograph error codes rather than simply reset and leave. Ask whether they can distinguish a failed microinverter from a gateway, cellular modem, or Wi-Fi dropout.
* **Can you check for NEM 2.0 status and the impact of any inverter swap?** Many San Diego systems remain on NEM 2.0; a like-for-like replacement can preserve the original rate treatment, while certain upgrades may trigger a new interconnection under a less favorable tariff. Ask them to document the current interconnection agreement, the remaining term of any grandfathered treatment, and the manufacturer warranty still in force.
* **Will you inspect AC and DC disconnects, the solar breaker, and CT placement before opening equipment?** Simple trips or loose CT clamps cause a large share of “dead system” calls. Confirm they start with non-invasive checks and that they will not open the inverter or main panel until those basics are ruled out.
* **Are you a licensed California electrical contractor for this work?** Inverter and meter-side diagnostics involve energized equipment. Ask for the license number, whether the person on site is the qualifying individual or a supervised employee, and whether they carry brand-specific diagnostic tools for the hardware on your roof.
* **What is included in the diagnostic visit price?** Get trip charge, diagnosis-only vs. repair, parts availability, and any follow-up warranty in writing. Ask whether they leave a written fault report you can use with the manufacturer, the original installer, or SDG&E if a meter accuracy question arises.
* **Will you leave documentation of codes, photos, and recommended next steps?** Keep records for warranty claims, utility disputes, or future service. Ask for clear photos of status lights, error screens, CT orientation if accessible, and the nameplate data of the inverter or gateway.

## Why San Diego systems need specialized diagnostics

San Diego’s early solar boom left a large installed base of systems that are now past the original installer warranty window. Homeowners commonly see production that is 20% or more below the same month last year with no new shade, red or orange inverter status lights, individual microinverters that stop reporting in the monitoring app, or utility bills that no longer show the expected export credits under net metering.

Many solar sales organizations focus on new footprints and multi-family projects and treat residential troubleshooting as low priority. That leaves owners of underperforming arrays waiting while the system sits offline or silently under-produces. Specialized diagnostic technicians who carry brand-specific tools, understand SDG&E net-metering data, and can document whether a problem is the inverter, the production meter or current transformers (CTs), the gateway, or the utility meter itself close this gap.

Local context that matters: SDG&E smart meters record bi-directional flow for net energy metering. Comparing the inverter monitoring portal to the utility’s export readings is one of the fastest ways to separate a true production failure from a metering or communication fault. A system that looks healthy in the app but shows weak export on the bill may have a CT polarity or placement issue; a system that is offline in the app but still exporting may have only a communication problem. Always use a licensed electrician for work inside the inverter, AC disconnect, or main panel. Do not open equipment yourself.

Before the visit, note the inverter status light color, any on-screen error codes, and recent production numbers from the monitoring app. Photograph the smart meter face if it cycles through export readings. When a technician proposes a repair or replacement, ask explicitly whether the work preserves any remaining NEM 2.0 treatment and whether a new interconnection application will be required. Like-for-like inverter swaps on the same capacity and configuration are often treated differently from capacity increases or major redesigns. Get that assessment in writing before authorizing hardware changes.

Also ask how they handle partial failures: one or two dead microinverters on an otherwise healthy array, a string inverter that faults only in heat, or a gateway that loses cellular service after a plan lapse. The right answer is not always “replace everything.” A clear diagnostic report lets you decide whether to claim under a manufacturer warranty, negotiate with the original installer if any labor warranty remains, or pay for targeted repair.

## What good documentation looks like

A useful diagnostic package usually includes: date and weather conditions; inverter and gateway model/serial numbers; monitoring-app screenshots for the same period as utility export data; error codes and status light descriptions; photos of disconnects, breakers, and any accessible CT or meter wiring; a clear statement of the most likely root cause; and a written price for the recommended next step (repair, module-level replacement, gateway/modem work, or further utility meter testing). Keep that package with your interconnection paperwork and warranty certificates.

If the utility meter itself is suspected, the path is usually a formal request to SDG&E for meter accuracy review, not a homeowner opening the meter base. Your diagnostic report becomes supporting evidence for that request.
