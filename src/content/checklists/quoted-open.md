Getting locked out of your home or vehicle in Houston—whether in Montrose, the Heights, Memorial, or near George Bush Intercontinental Airport—creates immediate stress. Unfortunately, emergency lockout calls are the #1 target for deceptive lead-generation call centers that advertise ridiculously low rates on search engines, then extort vulnerable homeowners on site.

The classic scam follows a predictable pattern: an ad promises a "$15 to $29 service call." When the unmarked car arrives, an untrained subcontractor claims your standard deadbolt is a *"high-security commercial lock that cannot be picked."* They pull out a cordless drill, destroy your lock in seconds, and hand you an unexpected bill for $350 to $650 in cash or peer-to-peer mobile payment.

Protect yourself before you authorize dispatch with this non-negotiable Houston locksmith checklist.

## 1. Demand a binding "Total Quoted-Open Price" over the phone

Never allow a locksmith to dispatch based solely on a "service call fee" or "trip charge."

- **Ask:** *"What is the total out-the-door price to unlock a standard residential front door deadbolt, including service call, labor, mileage, and taxes?"*
- **Listen for evasion:** If the dispatcher says, *"It starts at $29, but the technician has to evaluate the lock on site to give a price,"* hang up immediately. Standard residential residential locks (Kwikset, Schlage, Defiant) are universally recognizable, and an experienced locksmith knows the exact labor cost to pick them.
- **The rule:** Insist on a firm price range (e.g., $90 to $150 total). If they refuse to give a price cap, call another provider.

## 2. Verify Texas Department of Public Safety (DPS) Private Security licensing

In Texas, all locksmith companies and individual technicians are legally required to be licensed under the Texas Department of Public Safety (DPS) Regulatory Services Division (Private Security Program).

- **Ask:** *"What is your Texas DPS company license number, and will the technician display their individual DPS pocket card upon arrival?"*
- **Verify on site:** Legitimate Texas locksmiths carry a plastic state-issued photo ID card with their name and license number. Unlicensed scam operators will make excuses or claim they are "exempt."
- You can verify license validity on the official [Texas Online Private Security (TOPS)](https://tops.esecurity.txdps.state.tx.us/) database.

## 3. Require non-destructive entry before anyone touches a drill

Drilling a lock should be an absolute last resort, used only for damaged internal mechanisms or ultra-high-security deadbolts (like Medeco or Mul-T-Lock).

- **Ask:** *"Do you attempt non-destructive entry (picking, bumping, or bypass) before drilling, and what happens if you cannot pick it?"*
- **Why it matters:** An experienced, licensed locksmith can pick 95% of standard residential pin-tumbler locks in 3 to 10 minutes without causing a single scratch to the lock or door frame. If the technician pulls a drill out of their bag within 30 seconds of arriving, stop them immediately. Drilling destroys the lock cylinder, forcing you to pay an inflated replacement fee for cheap hardware.

## 4. Legitimate locksmiths ask for proof of residency

A genuine professional will protect your security and their liability.
- Before opening the door, a legitimate locksmith will ask to see a government-issued photo ID showing the address, or utility bills/lease documents immediately once inside.
- If a technician arrives and starts opening doors without asking for proof that you live there, they are operating illegally and putting neighborhood security at risk.

## 5. Agree on payment methods before work begins

- Confirm that the company accepts credit cards. Scammers frequently demand cash or non-reversible peer-to-peer transfers (Cash App, Zelle) to avoid credit card chargebacks when customers discover they were overcharged.
- Always require an itemized printed or digital invoice clearly showing the company’s legal name, Texas DPS license number, labor, and hardware costs.
