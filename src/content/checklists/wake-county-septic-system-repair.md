A septic failure can turn into an urgent and expensive problem. Sewage odors, wet ground, slow drains, surfacing effluent, or a pump alarm may point to a problem with the tank, pump, distribution box, drainfield, plumbing, or soil conditions. The first contractor who arrives may pump the tank or replace a component, but that does not automatically mean the underlying failure has been diagnosed.

Wake County is a defensible market for this checklist because its December 2025 One Water Plan reports approximately 80,000 permitted septic systems in the county. A county public-health presentation also describes roughly 17% of the county's population as relying on septic systems. These are countywide figures, not a count of failing systems. They show that septic repair is a meaningful local homeowner service, especially where a property is not connected to public sewer.

The work is regulated. North Carolina law requires appropriate certification for contractors who construct, install, or repair regulated on-site wastewater systems. State law generally requires an Improvement Permit and Construction Authorization before repair work begins, and a system cannot be covered or put back into use until the required inspection or verification is complete. Routine maintenance is treated differently from repair, so ask the county to classify the proposed work before agreeing to a scope.

## 1. Get a diagnosis before accepting a replacement proposal

- **Ask:** *"What failed, what evidence supports that diagnosis, and what lower-cost repair options did you rule out?"*
- **Request:** Written inspection report, photographs, tank and pump findings, alarm history, drainfield observations, and available septic permit or as-built records.

A septic problem is not always a failed drainfield. A blocked outlet filter, damaged baffle, failed pump, electrical issue, broken line, or saturated field can produce overlapping symptoms. Pumping may temporarily reduce symptoms without fixing the cause. Ask whether pumping is needed for inspection, whether a camera or other test is appropriate, and whether the soil or drainfield needs evaluation.

If the contractor recommends replacing the whole system, ask for evidence supporting that conclusion. A credible proposal should identify failed components and explain why repair is not feasible or permitted. Do not authorize excavation based only on a verbal statement that the system is "shot."

## 2. Verify the contractor's North Carolina certification

- **Ask:** *"What North Carolina on-site wastewater contractor certification level do you hold, and does it cover the system and repair proposed?"*
- **Request:** Contractor certification details, proof of general liability coverage, workers' compensation information, and subcontractor details.
- **Check:** Confirm credentials with the North Carolina On-Site Water Protection program and Wake County Environmental Services.

North Carolina General Statute 90A-72 requires a person constructing, installing, or repairing a regulated on-site wastewater system to be certified at the required level for the specified system. The statute also addresses certified inspectors and evaluators. Do not accept a general plumbing license as a substitute for septic-system certification when the work includes the tank, treatment system, dispersal field, or related components governed by septic rules.

Ask who actually supervises the work. If a company sells the project but hires another crew to excavate or install the system, identify the responsible certified contractor in the written agreement.

## 3. Confirm the permit path before digging

- **Ask:** *"Has Wake County determined whether this is maintenance or a permitted repair, and who will obtain the Improvement Permit and Construction Authorization if required?"*
- **Request:** Copies of the application, permit, approved repair plan, site conditions, and any written county determination.

North Carolina General Statute 130A-336 generally prohibits beginning installation, construction, or repair of a wastewater system without the required Improvement Permit and Construction Authorization, while routine maintenance does not require those permits. The distinction matters. A contractor should not label a substantive repair as "maintenance" simply to avoid the permit process.

Wake County's Onsite Water Protection staff handle wastewater repair permitting and technical assistance. Ask the county what records exist for your parcel and whether the proposed scope requires a site visit, soil evaluation, repair plan, or other approval. If the property is close to a well, stream, property line, building, or easement, make sure those constraints are considered before the contractor marks an excavation area.

## 4. Do not let the crew cover work before required inspection

- **Ask:** *"Who requests the inspection, and will the county or authorized evaluator inspect the repair before the system is covered or returned to service?"*
- **Request:** Inspection approval, written verification where an authorized alternative process is used, as-built drawing, and final Operation Permit or other county-issued closeout record.

North Carolina General Statute 130A-337 says a wastewater system may not be covered or placed into use until the required inspection or verification confirms the installation or repair follows the permit and applicable rules. The statute provides certain alternative verification pathways involving authorized evaluators, engineers, or private compliance inspectors when statutory conditions are met.

Do not rely on a contractor's phone photo as a substitute for required official approval. Ask for the record showing the repair was accepted and what conditions, if any, remain.

## 5. Compare complete bids and warranties

- **Ask:** *"Does the price include diagnosis, permits, soil work, excavation, tank or pump components, piping, electrical work, backfill, restoration, inspection, and cleanup?"*
- **Request:** Itemized estimate, material and equipment specifications, schedule, warranty terms, and written change-order process.

A quote may exclude difficult access, tree or pavement removal, soil replacement, electrical service, landscaping, or disposal. Ask whether the contractor is pricing a specific repair or offering an allowance that could expand once the ground is opened. Get written approval before extra work begins.

Ask what warranty applies to the tank, pump, controls, piping, labor, and drainfield work. A warranty covering a component but excluding labor, excavation, or diagnosis may be less useful than it sounds. Keep the approved design and final inspection record because future repairs or a property sale may depend on them.

## Warning signs

- The contractor recommends full replacement without explaining the diagnosis.
- A salesperson says no permit is needed but cannot explain why the work qualifies as maintenance.
- The company will not provide required septic certification details.
- The contractor wants to cover the repair before required inspection or written verification.
- The estimate omits excavation, backfill, electrical work, disposal, restoration, or permit costs.
- The company promises a guaranteed drainfield cure without site-specific evidence.
- The contractor will not put warranties, exclusions, and change orders in writing.

## Before you book

- Find your existing septic permit, system layout, and Operation Permit if available.
- Ask Wake County whether the proposed work is maintenance or a permitted repair.
- Verify the contractor's certification level and insurance.
- Get a written diagnosis with photographs and the reason for the proposed fix.
- Confirm who obtains permits and who requests the inspection.
- Compare itemized bids with the same scope and exclusions.
- Do not let the system be covered or reused before required approval.
- Keep permits, inspection records, as-built drawings, invoices, and warranties.

If the property is outside Wake County, the local health department and local rules may differ. Verify current requirements for the exact parcel with the county environmental health office before work begins.
