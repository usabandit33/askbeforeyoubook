Melbourne's restaurant corridors along US-1, Wickham Road, and the surrounding Space Coast support a growing number of commercial kitchens. Grease vapor from fryers, grills, and charbroilers accumulates inside hoods, filters, ducts, and rooftop fans. When that buildup is not removed on schedule, the system becomes a fire hazard and a compliance liability. Local fire officials can shut a kitchen down when current cleaning documentation is missing.

Florida enforces commercial kitchen exhaust cleaning through the Florida Fire Prevention Code, which adopts NFPA 96 (Standard for Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations). The standard sets inspection and cleaning frequency by cooking volume and fuel type, requires cleaning to bare metal by trained and certified personnel acceptable to the authority having jurisdiction, and demands written documentation.

## 1. Know your required cleaning frequency under NFPA 96

NFPA 96 ties the inspection schedule to how the kitchen operates:

- Monthly for solid-fuel cooking (wood, charcoal, pellets).
- Quarterly for high-volume operations, including 24-hour kitchens, heavy charbroiling, and wok cooking.
- Semiannually for moderate-volume cooking.
- Annually for low-volume or seasonal operations such as many churches and day camps.

These are minimums. The local fire marshal (the Authority Having Jurisdiction) can require more frequent service based on observed conditions. The schedule is for inspection; when grease exceeds the allowed thickness, the system must be cleaned.

**Ask:** "Based on our cooking volume and fuel type, what NFPA 96 frequency applies, and can you document the last cleaning date and areas covered?"

Do not rely on a generic "every three months" claim without confirming it matches your actual operation and the local fire authority's expectations.

## 2. Confirm the company and technicians are acceptable to the fire authority

NFPA 96 requires that grease-contaminated systems be cleaned by properly trained, qualified, and certified persons who are acceptable to the Authority Having Jurisdiction. Many operators look for technicians affiliated with recognized training programs such as those associated with the International Kitchen Exhaust Cleaning Association (IKECA) or equivalent documented training.

**Ask:** "Who will perform the work, what training or certification do they hold, and can you show recent examples of the documentation you leave for fire inspectors in Brevard County?"

Verify that the company carries liability insurance appropriate for commercial kitchen work and that the people on site understand access panels, fan cleaning, and the requirement to clean to bare metal—not merely wipe visible surfaces.

## 3. Require access to the entire grease path

A compliant cleaning reaches the hood, filters, plenum, horizontal and vertical ducts, and the exhaust fan. Hidden sections that cannot be accessed cannot be verified as clean. NFPA 96 expects proper access doors and a report of any areas that could not be reached.

**Ask:** "Will your crew clean the full grease path including ducts and the rooftop fan, and will the report list any inaccessible sections?"

If the system lacks adequate access panels, ask whether the company will install grease-tight access doors as part of the scope or as a separate recommendation. Surface-only cleaning of the visible hood will not satisfy a thorough inspection.

## 4. Demand usable documentation for the fire marshal and insurer

After each cleaning, the company should provide:

- A dated sticker or certificate on the hood showing the company name, date, and areas cleaned.
- A written report (often with before-and-after photographs) describing the work performed and any deficiencies found.
- Confirmation that the system was cleaned to the applicable standard.

**Ask:** "What exact documents will I receive the same day, and are they the format local fire inspectors in Melbourne and Brevard County typically accept?"

Keep the reports on site and with your insurance records. Missing or incomplete documentation is a common reason kitchens fail routine fire inspections.

## 5. Separate hood cleaning from suppression-system service

Hood and duct grease cleaning (NFPA 96) is different from the semiannual inspection and service of the fire-suppression system under NFPA 17A. Fusible links and other detection components have their own replacement schedules. Do not assume a single vendor automatically covers both, and do not treat a suppression tag as proof that the exhaust system was cleaned.

**Ask:** "Does this proposal cover only exhaust cleaning, or does it also include suppression-system inspection and link replacement? Who is responsible for each?"

Clarify the distinction in writing so you maintain both sets of required records.

## 6. Compare complete scopes and after-hours logistics

A useful commercial hood-cleaning proposal should state:

- The frequency recommended for your specific cooking volume.
- Whether the price includes filters, ducts, fan, and rooftop components.
- Access-panel work if needed.
- Documentation package (sticker, written report, photos).
- Scheduling (many kitchens require after-hours or early-morning service).
- Any additional charges for excessive grease, difficult access, or multiple stories.

**Ask:** "What is excluded, and how are change orders handled if the crew finds heavier buildup or missing access?"

Get the agreement in writing. Confirm whether the company will coordinate with your staff for filter handling and kitchen readiness.

## Warning signs

- The company cannot explain which NFPA 96 frequency applies to your kitchen.
- The quote covers only the visible hood and filters, with no mention of ducts or the fan.
- No sample of the written report or sticker is available.
- The technicians cannot describe bare-metal cleaning or access requirements.
- The price is dramatically lower than other bids without a clear difference in scope.
- The company pressures you to skip documentation "because the fire marshal never asks."
- Suppression-system service is mixed into the cleaning price without a separate qualified technician.

## Before you book

Identify the correct NFPA 96 frequency for your cooking volume, verify that the cleaning company and technicians are acceptable to the local fire authority, require full-path cleaning and same-day documentation, and keep the reports ready for inspection. Confirm current expectations with the City of Melbourne Fire Department or the applicable Brevard County fire authority for your address. This is general compliance guidance only; it is not a substitute for the advice of your fire marshal or a qualified fire-protection professional.
