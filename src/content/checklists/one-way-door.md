Hearing thumping, scratching, or chattering in your Chicago attic late at night or early in the morning is alarming. In historic brick bungalows, two-flats, and Victorian homes across Chicago—from Lincoln Square and Portage Park to Beverly—mature trees and aging fascia boards provide ideal entry points for raccoons, gray squirrels, and bats seeking warm nesting shelter.

Many pest companies sell recurring "trapping packages," setting wire cage traps on the lawn or roof. Trapping is often ineffective and inhumane: it frequently catches non-target neighborhood animals while leaving orphaned babies trapped inside the attic to die behind walls.

Before hiring an animal control company in Chicago, use this checklist to require humane one-way exclusion doors and permanent structural sealing.

## 1. Demand one-way exclusion doors instead of open-ended cage trapping

Humane wildlife management prioritizes letting the animals exit on their own while physically preventing them from getting back inside.

- **Ask:** *"Do you install a one-way exclusion door (funnel device) over the primary entry hole, or do you rely on cage trapping?"*
- **How it works:** A one-way exclusion door is a specialized spring-loaded steel mesh gate fastened over the animal's exact entry hole. When the squirrel or raccoon exits at dusk to forage for food, the door swings shut behind them. They cannot re-enter.
- **Why it matters:** Trapping on the ground only removes individual animals without solving the problem. Unless the structural hole on the roof is sealed with heavy-gauge steel, new wildlife will reoccupy the scent-marked attic within days.

## 2. Check for newborn babies before installing exclusion doors

During spring and summer (March through July in Illinois), female raccoons and squirrels enter attics specifically to give birth.

- **Ask:** *"Will your technician physically enter the attic space to search for baby raccoons or squirrels before sealing the roof or installing a one-way door?"*
- **Critical risk:** If you install a one-way exclusion door while dependent baby animals are still nested in the insulation, the mother will frantically tear off roof shingles, gutters, and fascia trying to break back in to reach her litter. If she fails, the babies will die inside your walls, creating severe decomposition odors, biohazard conditions, and secondary fly infestations.
- **Humane removal:** A trained technician removes the babies by hand into an insulated reunion box outside, allowing the mother to relocate her young to a secondary den in the neighborhood.

## 3. Verify Illinois Department of Natural Resources (IDNR) licensing

In Illinois, commercial wildlife removal is regulated by state conservation law.

- **Ask:** *"Is the technician licensed by the Illinois Department of Natural Resources (IDNR) as a Nuisance Wildlife Control Operator (NWCO), and are they insured for roof work?"*
- Unlicensed operators often use poisons (which is strictly illegal for raccoons, squirrels, and bats) or fail to comply with Illinois wildlife relocation statutes.

## 4. Insist on heavy-gauge steel exclusion and roof sealing

Animals chew through wood, foam, and aluminum trim easily.
- **Ask:** *"What materials do you use to seal entry holes and secondary vulnerability points?"*
- **The standard:** All holes, gable vents, and roof returns must be reinforced with 16-gauge or 19-gauge galvanized steel hardware cloth (1/4-inch or 1/2-inch mesh) fastened with heavy-duty screws, not plastic caulk or spray foam.
- Chimney flues must be capped with commercial stainless steel spark arrestor caps.

## 5. Evaluate attic sanitation and biohazard cleanup scope

Raccoon feces can carry *Baylisascaris procyonis* (raccoon roundworm), whose microscopic eggs remain infectious in soil and insulation for years.
- If an animal lived in the attic for months, ask whether the company provides HEPA vacuuming, biohazard enzyme fogging, and targeted insulation replacement.
- Check whether your homeowner’s insurance policy covers wildlife remediation and attic insulation restoration under damage caused by wild animals.
