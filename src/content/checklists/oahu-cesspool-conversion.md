If your Oʻahu property has a cesspool, choosing a contractor is not simply a matter of replacing one underground tank with another. A compliant project may involve a licensed civil engineer, site evaluation, percolation testing, state plan approval, a licensed installer, closure of the old cesspool, construction inspection, and a final approval to use. The wrong sequence can leave you with a large bill and a system that is not approved.

Hawaiʻi law requires cesspools across the state to be upgraded, converted, or connected to a sewer system by January 1, 2050, subject to applicable exemptions. The Department of Health reports more than 11,000 cesspools on Oʻahu and approximately 88,000 statewide. It also identifies 43,000 cesspools statewide as posing a risk to water resources. This is a long-term statewide mandate, not a claim that every owner must replace a cesspool immediately. Some conditions can trigger an earlier upgrade requirement, so check your specific property and circumstances.

## 1. Confirm what wastewater system the property actually has

Do not rely only on an old listing, a seller's description, or a contractor's guess. Hawaiʻi's Department of Health explains that cesspools, septic tanks, aerobic treatment units, and sewer connections have different rules and maintenance requirements. The systems are often underground and can be misidentified in real-estate paperwork.

**Ask:** "What records show that this property has a cesspool, and can you confirm the system type before preparing a conversion proposal?"

Request any existing cesspool card, Department of Health records, property Tax Map Key (TMK), prior permits, pumping records, and inspection reports. If the records are incomplete, ask a licensed wastewater engineer how the system should be identified. The state also provides a cesspool prioritization tool that can help owners understand the relative priority assigned to a location. A priority ranking is not a contractor quote or a guarantee of funding.

## 2. Compare a sewer connection with an approved individual wastewater system

The right solution depends on whether a municipal sewer connection is available and whether the lot can support an individual wastewater system (IWS). An IWS may use a septic tank, an aerobic treatment unit, or another state-approved technology. Soil conditions, lot size, slope, groundwater, setbacks, access for equipment, and the distance to a sewer main can all affect feasibility and price.

**Ask:** "Have you compared a sewer connection with the IWS options that this property can legally and physically support?"

Do not let a contractor select an expensive system before the site has been evaluated. Ask the engineer to explain why the proposed option fits the property and what alternatives were ruled out. If a sewer connection is possible, request the written connection requirements and a separate cost estimate. If an IWS is proposed, ask whether a percolation test and survey have been completed and whether the design accounts for the property's soil and water-table conditions.

## 3. Hire the engineer before committing to construction

The Department of Health's homeowner process starts with a Hawaiʻi-licensed civil engineer. The engineer evaluates the site, prepares plans, and submits them to the Wastewater Branch for review and approval. The state lists an application form, owner certification, and site evaluation among the plan-submission documents.

**Ask:** "Who is the licensed civil engineer responsible for the design, and will that engineer monitor construction and submit the final inspection report?"

Check the engineer's license class and current status through the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA). Get a written engineering agreement that explains the site work, design, plan submission, revisions, construction monitoring, and final documents. Ask who pays if the state requests changes to the plans and whether the fee includes those revisions.

## 4. Verify the contractor's exact license class

After the plans are approved, the Department of Health says the IWS must be installed by a licensed contractor holding one of the following license classes: A, C-9, C-37, C-37a, or C-43. Depending on the work, a licensed plumber or electrician may also be needed. The state warns that a contractor holding a different license class will not be accepted for the required installation.

**Ask:** "Which license class authorizes your company to perform this exact scope, and can I verify it in the DCCA license search?"

Check the legal company name, license number, classification, and status yourself. Do not assume a general business registration, a sanitation service license, or a contractor's experience on another kind of project is enough. If the project includes electrical or plumbing work, ask which licensed subcontractor will perform it and who is responsible for coordinating the work.

## 5. Compare complete written bids, not just the tank or equipment price

A useful proposal should separate engineering, testing, permits, materials, construction, old cesspool closure, inspections, and site restoration. Ask each bidder to price the same scope so you can compare like with like.

A Hawaiʻi Public Radio report dated June 25, 2026 quoted an industry expert estimating that the state was converting about 400 cesspools a year and that a single conversion could cost roughly $25,000 to $50,000. These are reported estimates, not a state price schedule or a guaranteed Oʻahu quote. Your site's soil, access, system design, and distance from a sewer connection can change the cost substantially.

Ask each bidder to identify:

- Site evaluation, survey, and percolation testing.
- Engineering and plan preparation.
- Department of Health review and permit fees.
- The new system, equipment, excavation, piping, and electrical work.
- Pumping, closure, and backfilling of the old cesspool.
- Construction monitoring and final inspection.
- Landscaping, driveway, concrete, and other restoration.
- What happens if rock, groundwater, access problems, or unsuitable soil changes the design.

**Ask:** "What is excluded from this price, and what written approval is required before any extra work is billed?"

## 6. Do not confuse a loan announcement with money already approved

The Department of Health's financial-support page currently lists a statewide 2026 Cesspool Conversion Revolving Loan Program, but says the program is under development and will be administered by the Hawaiʻi Green Infrastructure Authority. Do not assume applications are open or that your household qualifies. The same page lists prior programs separately, including a completed 2023 pilot grant. A past grant is not a current entitlement.

**Ask:** "Which specific program is open today, who administers it, and can I verify my eligibility directly with that agency before signing?"

Get the program rules in writing. Confirm whether an award must be approved before construction, which costs are eligible, how reimbursements are paid, and what happens if the project changes. Do not pay a contractor an extra fee for a promised grant or loan that the agency has not approved.

## 7. Make final approval part of the contract

The engineer should monitor the construction. At the end, the engineer must submit a final construction inspection report with the contractor certification form to the Wastewater Branch. The state says it cannot issue the final approval to use the new IWS unless those documents are submitted and there are no unresolved discrepancies.

**Ask:** "Who is responsible for obtaining the final approval to use, and will I receive the complete closeout package?"

The contract should identify who submits the final paperwork, who corrects deficiencies, and who pays for reinspection or corrective work if the installation does not match the approved plans. Keep the approved plans, permits, contractor certification, engineer's report, cesspool closure documentation, and final approval with your property records.

## Warning signs

- The contractor proposes a system before a licensed engineer evaluates the property.
- The company cannot show the required DCCA license class and current status.
- The quote excludes engineering, testing, state approval, or final inspection without explaining who handles them.
- The contractor says a 2050 deadline means no rules can apply earlier.
- A salesperson promises a grant or loan that the state agency has not approved.
- The contractor asks for a large payment before plans are approved or refuses milestone-based billing.
- The company says the job is complete but cannot provide the engineer's final report and state approval to use.

## Before you book

Confirm the existing system and TMK, check the property's priority information, and compare sewer connection with IWS options. Hire a licensed civil engineer to evaluate and design the project. After plan approval, verify the installer holds an accepted license class. Use a detailed contract that includes the old cesspool closure, site restoration, engineer monitoring, and final approval to use. Verify current rules, permits, funding availability, and license status directly with Hawaiʻi's Department of Health, DCCA, and the relevant county agencies.
