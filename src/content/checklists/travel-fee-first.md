When urgent legal, medical, or real estate documents must be executed at a home, hospital bedside (such as OHSU, Providence St. Vincent, or Legacy Emanuel), or assisted living facility in Portland, hiring a traveling mobile notary public provides essential convenience.

However, consumers are frequently shocked when an invoice intended for a few notarized signatures totals $150 to $250. Under Oregon law, while the fee for the physical stamp is strictly capped by statute, traveling notaries are legally permitted to charge a travel fee—provided the fee is agreed upon in advance.

Before dispatching a mobile notary in Multnomah, Washington, or Clackamas County, use this checklist to lock in total fees and avoid unexpected travel surcharges.

## 1. Differentiate the Oregon statutory notary fee from the travel fee

Under Oregon law (ORS 194.400 and Oregon Administrative Rule OAR 160-100-0400), notary fees are legally capped:

- **Statutory Stamp Fee:** An Oregon notary public may charge a maximum of **$10 per notarial act** (per signature/acknowledgment or jurat). Charging more than $10 per stamp is a state violation subject to disciplinary action by the Oregon Secretary of State.
- **Mobile Travel Fee (OAR 160-100-0420):** A notary may charge a separate travel fee, but **only if the signer agrees to the fee in advance**. The travel fee can be billed as the federal IRS standard mileage rate or a flat negotiated travel fee.
- **Ask:** *"What is your exact flat travel fee to my specific address, and does it include parking, tolls, and up to 30 minutes of appointment time?"*

## 2. Clarify wait-time charges for Portland hospital or care facility visits

Bedside notarizations at Portland-area care facilities often encounter delays with nurses, doctors, or medication schedules.

- **Ask:** *"Do you charge an hourly wait-time fee if the signing is delayed by hospital staff, and what is the grace period before extra charges begin?"*
- Clear agreements state how long the notary will wait (e.g., 15 to 20 minutes included) and what incremental fee applies per 15-minute block thereafter.

## 3. Verify acceptable government-issued photo identification

A mobile notary cannot legally complete a signing if the signer cannot prove their identity.

- **Acceptable IDs in Oregon:** Valid, unexpired government-issued photo ID (Oregon driver’s license, out-of-state driver’s license, US Passport, military ID, or federally recognized tribal ID).
- **Expired ID rules:** Under Oregon law, an ID can be accepted if it has not expired more than 3 years before the notarial act, provided it contains a photograph and signature.
- **Credible witness alternative:** If an elderly or hospitalized family member has lost their ID, Oregon law permits identification via one or two "credible witnesses" who have valid IDs and can swear under oath to the signer’s identity. Confirm this with the notary ahead of time.

## 4. Confirm witness requirements for Powers of Attorney and healthcare directives

A notary cannot act as a witness on a document they are notarizing.

- **Ask:** *"Does my document require independent third-party witnesses (such as a Will, Living Trust, or Advance Directive), and can your company provide qualified witnesses for an extra fee?"*
- In hospitals and hospice facilities, hospital nurses and doctors are strictly forbidden by hospital policy from serving as legal witnesses on patient legal documents. You must arrange for independent, non-family, non-beneficiary witnesses to be present.

## 5. Verify the notary’s active commission with the Oregon Secretary of State

- Request the notary’s full legal name and commission number.
- You can instantly verify their commission status and expiration date using the official [Oregon Secretary of State Notary Search](https://sos.oregon.gov/business/Pages/notary.aspx) database.
