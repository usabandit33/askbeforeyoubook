Seattle tree work is not just a matter of finding the cheapest crew with a chainsaw. Depending on the tree's location, size, species, tier, and relation to a construction project, the job may require a city permit, public notice, a registered tree-service provider, a qualified tree-risk assessor, traffic control, or restoration work. A contractor who says "we can take care of the paperwork" without identifying the exact permit and registration may be leaving you with the legal and financial risk.

Seattle is a strong market for this checklist because the city regulates both private-property and street trees. Seattle says most private-property tree removal requires approval from the Department of Construction and Inspections, while street trees are regulated by the Seattle Department of Transportation. The city also says commercial tree work on private property must be performed by a registered tree-service provider. Unpermitted removal can trigger stop-work orders, penalties, and expensive disputes over what should have happened before the tree came down.

The city's provider directory also warns that it only confirms registration status. It does not endorse a company or accept liability for the work. That distinction matters. Registration is a screening step, not a substitute for checking insurance, references, scope, and safety practices.

## 1. Confirm the tree's location and regulatory category

- **Ask:** *"Is this tree on private property, in the public right-of-way, or part of a development project, and which city department has jurisdiction?"*
- **Request:** The address, tree species if known, trunk diameter at standard height, photographs, and any existing permit or public notice.
- **Check:** Use Seattle's tree services portal and maps to determine whether the tree is regulated.

Seattle's rules differ for private trees, street trees, park trees, and trees associated with construction or demolition. The city says a permit is required for street-tree removal and that private-property tree removal is generally regulated by SDCI. Some reportable pruning and root work also require notice. If the work is part of a building project, the tree rules may connect to the construction permit.

Do not let a contractor decide that a tree is "not protected" based only on a quick glance. Ask for the exact rule or city confirmation supporting that conclusion.

## 2. Verify the company is registered for the work

- **Ask:** *"What is your current Seattle Tree Service Provider registration number, and is it active for the department that governs this job?"*
- **Request:** A copy of the registration, the name of the qualified person who will assess the tree, proof of general liability insurance, workers' compensation coverage, and any subcontractor information.

Seattle says commercial tree work on private property must be performed by an SDCI-registered provider. Work in the right-of-way requires SDOT registration and may also require a right-of-way use permit or traffic-control review. The city directory says registration status can take up to 48 hours to refresh after a renewal, so check close to the scheduled start date.

If the contractor says a registration is unnecessary, ask whether the job is truly routine pruning or whether it includes reportable work, removal, consulting, or work on a regulated tree.

## 3. Ask whether the tree should be removed at all

- **Ask:** *"What evidence shows removal is necessary, and what alternatives did you consider?"*
- **Request:** A written risk assessment, photographs of defects, decay or structural issues, and a proposal for pruning, cabling, monitoring, or removal.

Seattle's provider directory notes that only providers with a current Tree Risk Assessment Qualification may prepare tree-risk assessments for hazardous-tree removal. Ask who prepared the assessment and whether that person has the credential. A crew that wants to remove a large tree without documenting the hazard may be acting for convenience rather than necessity.

For a tree near a home, power line, road, neighbor's property, or retaining wall, ask how the crew will control the drop zone and protect structures. Get a written explanation of the rigging plan, access route, and who pays if a fence, roof, pavement, or utility line is damaged.

## 4. Put permits, public notices, and traffic control in the contract

- **Ask:** *"Who will obtain the permit, post the required public notice, and pay for any right-of-way or traffic-control costs?"*
- **Request:** Permit numbers, public-notice dates, traffic-control plans if applicable, and a written schedule.

Seattle says reportable private-property work requires public notice and that tree-removal notices may need to be posted for multiple business days before work. Street-tree work may require SDOT permits and a right-of-way use permit. Ask for copies before the crew arrives.

Do not assume the contractor's price includes traffic control, sidewalk protection, parking, crane staging, or restoration. Those details can turn a simple quote into a much larger bill.

## 5. Compare the complete scope

- **Ask:** *"Does the price include removal, stump grinding, hauling, logs, chips, raking, soil replacement, replanting, and all site repairs?"*
- **Request:** A line-item estimate, equipment list, cleanup standard, and change-order process.

Some crews leave logs or chips unless the customer specifically asks for removal. Others quote stump grinding separately. A good contract says whether the job includes root flare cleanup, topsoil, reseeding, fence repair, driveway protection, and restoration of the planting strip.

Require written approval before extra work. If the crew discovers a hidden utility, unstable trunk, or access problem, ask for photos and a revised price.

## Warning signs

- No current Seattle registration number or no proof of insurance.
- The contractor says permits are unnecessary without identifying the rule.
- The company cannot explain whether the tree is private, street, park, or development-related.
- No written risk assessment for a hazardous-tree claim.
- A verbal promise that "cleanup is included" with no definition.
- Pressure to pay a large deposit before permits or a site plan exist.
- The bid excludes traffic control, stump work, or property-damage responsibility.

## Before you book

- Confirm the tree's location, size, species, and regulatory category.
- Verify the provider in Seattle's current directory.
- Check whether SDCI or SDOT approval and public notice are required.
- Request a written risk assessment or explanation of alternatives.
- Compare line-item bids for removal, rigging, stump work, hauling, cleanup, and restoration.
- Confirm insurance and who pays for damage.
- Keep permits, notices, photos, invoices, and the final cleanup record.

Seattle's rules can change with the exact tree and project. Verify current requirements with SDCI or SDOT for the property address before work begins.
