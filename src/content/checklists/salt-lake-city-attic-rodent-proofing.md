Standard pest control companies often set traps or bait stations while leaving structural entry gaps open. General contractors frequently refuse dusty, insulation-choked attic sealing work. Use these questions before you book permanent physical exclusion.

## Questions to ask before you book

* **Will you perform a full inspection of the roofline, soffits, gable vents, pipe penetrations, and foundation gaps?** Entry points can be as small as a quarter-inch for mice. Ask how thoroughly they search and whether they map every gap, including less obvious routes around chimneys and utility penetrations.
* **Do you seal with chew-proof materials (hardware cloth / steel mesh)?** Confirm they replace flimsy plastic screens and close gaps with materials rodents cannot chew through—steel mesh, metal flashing, or approved sealants—not expandable foam alone.
* **Do you address tree and vegetation access?** Ask whether they recommend or include trimming overhanging limbs (typically 6–8 feet from the roofline) that give squirrels and other climbers an easy path onto the roof.
* **Is cleanup and sanitization of contaminated insulation included?** Droppings and pheromone trails attract new animals; ask what is included in the scope, whether framing is treated, and how damaged insulation is handled.
* **Is this exclusion work, or only trapping?** Clarify that the goal is permanent sealing of entry points, not just catch-and-release or monthly bait stations. Recurring infestations usually mean the house was never closed.
* **Are you properly licensed and insured for this work?** Ask about Utah Department of Agriculture and Food (UDAF) pest-control licensing when pesticides are used, and about liability coverage for attic and roof work. Confirm they are equipped for confined, insulated spaces.
* **What is the written scope and warranty?** Get the list of entry points sealed, materials used, and any follow-up guarantee in writing. Ask what conditions void the warranty (new openings from remodeling, storm damage, or homeowner alterations).

## Securing Salt Lake City homes against attic wildlife

Salt Lake City and the broader Wasatch Front combine cold winters, expanding suburbs, and older housing stock with plenty of gaps. When temperatures fall, mice, voles, and occasional squirrels seek warm shelter in attics. Once inside, they chew wiring, shred insulation, and leave contamination that can affect indoor air quality and create fire risk.

The trap-only cycle frustrates many homeowners: technicians catch the current animals but leave the structural openings that invited them. Permanent exclusion—sealing every practical gap with chew-proof materials, addressing vegetation bridges, and cleaning as needed—stops the cycle rather than managing it month after month.

Local context that matters: Utah regulates commercial pest control through the Department of Agriculture and Food. Confirm that any company applying pesticides holds the appropriate UDAF credentials, and ask about insurance for attic and roof work. Local building and health guidance consistently emphasizes sealing openings and removing access and harborage—not relying on traps alone. Use licensed, insured technicians for attic work; do not walk on or disturb contaminated insulation without proper protection.

## Seasonal timing along the Wasatch Front

Activity often spikes in fall as outdoor temperatures drop and again in early spring when animals seek nesting sites. Booking exclusion before peak pressure reduces the chance of finding an active nest mid-job. If you already hear scratching or find fresh droppings, prioritize inspection and exclusion over waiting for a “convenient” season—delaying only extends damage and contamination.

Ask whether the company offers a follow-up visit after the first cold snap or after the initial sealing, when animals may test newly closed routes. A short warranty window with a documented re-check is more useful than a long verbal promise with no scheduled verification.
