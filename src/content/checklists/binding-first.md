Moving quotes can be difficult to compare because companies may price the same move in different ways. One mover may offer an hourly rate, another may provide a flat-price proposal, and a third may estimate the cost based on the shipment's weight or the services requested.

The important question is not just which quote is lowest. It is what the price covers, what could make it change, and what you may be required to pay on moving day.

Austin residents should first establish whether the move is within Texas or crosses state lines. Different rules apply to intrastate and interstate moves, so the terms on one type of estimate should not automatically be assumed to apply to the other.

## 1. Binding versus non-binding estimates

### Binding estimates

A binding estimate is a written agreement that sets the price for the shipment and services described in the estimate, subject to the terms and applicable rules. For interstate moves, the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) explains that a binding estimate generally means the mover cannot require payment above the estimated amount at delivery for the listed quantities and services.

That protection depends on the agreed scope. If you add furniture, boxes, packing services, or other work, the original estimate may not cover those additions. Before the move, ask how any changes will be documented and whether a revised written estimate is needed.

A binding estimate can make budgeting easier because the price is defined in advance. However, it is only useful if the inventory and service description are accurate. If the estimate omits a storage unit, garage, bulky furniture, or packing work, you may have a disagreement about what the original price included.

### Non-binding estimates

A non-binding estimate is an estimate of the expected cost, not a guaranteed final price. For interstate moves, the final charge can depend on factors such as actual shipment weight, services performed, and the mover's applicable tariff.

Under federal rules for interstate household-goods moves, the mover generally cannot require you to pay more than 110% of a non-binding estimate at delivery. The remaining balance, if properly due, may be collected later under the applicable rules. This is not a reason to ignore a non-binding estimate's terms: read the documents and understand how the final price will be calculated.

A non-binding estimate should still be reasonably accurate and based on the shipment and services described. If a mover gives you a rough figure without inspecting your belongings or asking meaningful questions, request a more complete written estimate.

Read the FMCSA's explanations of binding estimates and avoiding unexpected moving costs.

## 2. How hourly moving rates work

Hourly pricing is common for local moves, but the hourly rate alone does not tell you the total cost.

Ask what the rate includes:

- Number of movers
- Size and type of truck
- Minimum billable hours
- Travel time or a separate travel charge
- Fuel, supplies, and equipment
- Packing, wrapping, disassembly, and reassembly
- Stair, elevator, long-carry, or difficult-access charges
- Overtime, if applicable

Also ask when the clock starts and stops. A company may have a minimum charge or a specific policy for travel to and from its base. Confirm whether the estimate includes loading, driving, unloading, and any waiting time.

For example, two companies might advertise similar hourly rates, but one may include three movers and a truck while another charges separately for certain supplies or travel. Compare the expected total for the same job, not just the hourly number.

Ask the mover to estimate the number of hours based on your inventory, access conditions, and distance. Treat the estimate as a planning figure unless the written agreement expressly establishes a guaranteed or capped price.

## 3. Make sure the inventory is accurate

The inventory is one of the most important parts of a moving estimate. The mover needs a realistic picture of what is being transported and what services you want.

Include items in the garage, attic, storage unit, patio, and spare rooms. Mention large or unusually heavy items, such as safes, exercise equipment, oversized furniture, or appliances that need special handling. If you expect the movers to pack fragile items, disassemble furniture, or move belongings up or down stairs, make that clear before the estimate is prepared.

For a virtual or in-person survey, show the mover the entire shipment. Ask whether the estimate is based on the inventory and services you reviewed together. Keep a copy of the inventory and the estimate so you can identify any differences before loading begins.

Do not rely on a verbal assurance that an unlisted item will be included at no extra charge. Ask for the estimate or service agreement to be updated in writing.

## 4. Confirm the company and who will perform the move

Ask whether the business giving you the estimate will actually transport your belongings or whether it is acting as a broker arranging the move through another company.

If another mover will perform the work, ask for the carrier's name and contact details before committing. Make sure you understand whom you are paying, who is responsible for the shipment, and whom to contact if something goes wrong.

For interstate moves, use the FMCSA's Protect Your Move resources to learn about federal requirements and check relevant mover information. For an intrastate move within Texas, consult the Texas Department of Motor Vehicles' consumer guidance on moving.

Do not assume that a company's website, advertisement, or professional-looking estimate proves that it has the required authority. Verify the information relevant to the type of move you are making.

## 5. Understand the difference between a quote and a contract

A quote describes a proposed price. The contract and related moving documents establish the terms you agree to. Before signing, check that the documents identify the mover, pickup and delivery arrangements, services, pricing method, payment requirements, and the process for handling changes.

Ask these questions:

1. Is this estimate binding, non-binding, hourly, or subject to a maximum price?
2. What inventory and services does it cover?
3. Which fees could be added, and under what circumstances?
4. What happens if the move takes longer than expected?
5. What payment methods are accepted, and when is payment due?
6. How are delays, delivery changes, and claims for damage handled?

For interstate moves, federal requirements govern important parts of the estimating process. The FMCSA's estimating-charges guidance explains the rules for written estimates and related charges. Texas moves within the state may be subject to different requirements, so use Texas-specific guidance for an intrastate move.

Never sign blank forms or accept documents that contradict the price or services you were promised. Ask for corrections before the movers begin loading.

## 6. Watch for warning signs

Be cautious when a mover refuses to provide a written estimate, will not explain the pricing method, or quotes a very low price without understanding the size of the shipment.

Other warning signs include pressure to sign immediately, unclear company identity, unexplained changes to the estimate, and reluctance to put additional charges in writing. A broker that cannot identify the company expected to perform the move is another reason to pause and request clarification.

A low estimate is not necessarily a good deal if the inventory is incomplete or the charges are vague. Give each company the same information and compare written proposals using the same assumptions.

## 7. Choose the estimate that fits your move

A binding estimate may be attractive when the inventory and requested services are well defined and you want a more predictable price. A non-binding estimate may reflect a pricing structure based on actual weight and services, but you need to understand how the final charges are calculated. Hourly pricing can work for a local move when the scope is clear, the crew size is specified, and the billing rules are transparent.

No pricing format eliminates the need to read the paperwork. The best estimate is one that accurately describes your belongings, explains the services, identifies the company doing the work, and makes potential extra charges understandable before moving day.
