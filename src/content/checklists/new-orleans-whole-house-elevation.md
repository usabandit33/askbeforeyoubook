Raising a house in New Orleans is a major structural project, not an ordinary foundation repair. The home may need temporary support, engineered foundation work, a survey benchmark, utility disconnections and reconnections, new stairs or porches, and a city elevation permit. A quote that says only "raise house to required height" leaves too many important details unanswered.

The City of New Orleans says most of the city is below sea level and/or surrounded by flood levees, so the entire city is at risk of flooding. The city's hazard information also identifies Lakeview, Gentilly, and New Orleans East among the lower areas. That does not mean every property needs the same elevation or that raising a home is always the right solution. The appropriate project depends on the property's flood information, existing elevation, structure, foundation, insurance situation, and local approvals.

## 1. Confirm that whole-house elevation is the right project

First separate flood-risk mitigation from foundation settlement repair. Louisiana's house-raising rule defines raising as lifting a structure from its foundation to change its elevation or relocate it. It specifically excludes moving only part of a structure to level it because of settlement. Those are different jobs and can require different contractors and scopes.

**Ask:** "Are you proposing to raise the entire house for flood protection, or are you correcting settlement or foundation movement?"

Ask a qualified design professional to explain the problem, the proposed finished elevation, and the alternatives. Request the property's current elevation information, flood map details, and any existing elevation certificate. The City of New Orleans says it keeps elevation certificates submitted to Safety and Permits and makes them available to homeowners. If one is needed and is not on file, the city directs owners to a licensed land surveyor.

Do not accept a salesperson's statement that a certain height will automatically lower your flood insurance premium. Insurance pricing depends on the property, policy, flood zone, elevation information, and current rating rules. Ask your insurance agent what documentation is needed before treating a possible premium change as part of the project's financial return.

## 2. Verify the contractor's license classification for house raising

Louisiana has a specific rule for raising residential structures. When the value exceeds $7,500, only contractors holding one of the listed classifications may bid or perform the raising work: building construction; rigging, house moving, wrecking, and dismantling; foundations for buildings, equipment, or machinery; or foundations and pile driving. A residential construction license holder may bid or contract for raising, leveling, or shoring, but must contract with a properly classified contractor to perform the work.

**Ask:** "Which Louisiana State Licensing Board for Contractors classification authorizes the company that will physically raise my house?"

Get the legal business name, license number, and classification in writing. Check the company through the Louisiana State Licensing Board for Contractors' contractor search. If a general residential contractor will manage the project and use a specialist house-raising contractor, ask for the specialist's name and license too. Confirm that the actual contracting and subcontracting structure complies with the current state rule. Do not treat a city occupational license, a business registration, or a general claim of being insured as a substitute for the required contractor classification.

## 3. Require an engineered plan and a survey benchmark

The city's Structural Renovation / Elevation Permit page requires plans for structural work. For a whole-house elevation, the city specifically calls for engineered foundation drawings, a plot plan, a signed contract or approved estimate, and a Benchmark Certificate completed by a registered land surveyor on the city's form. The benchmark certificate must be dated within the previous six months.

**Ask:** "Who is the Louisiana-licensed architect or civil engineer responsible for the foundation design, and who is the registered land surveyor responsible for the benchmark?"

Request the engineer's sealed drawings and ask the engineer to explain the proposed foundation, piles or supports, connection details, and assumptions about the existing structure. Ask for the surveyor's benchmark certificate and make sure the finished elevation target is tied to the correct reference information for your property. Do not let a contractor substitute a verbal measurement or a sales brochure for engineered drawings and survey documentation.

## 4. Ask about riggers insurance and the complete permit package

New Orleans requires a Contractor's Riggers Insurance certificate or similar endorsement for a whole-house elevation. The city also requires engineered foundation drawings and says electrical and mechanical permits must be applied for before the elevation permit is released so that resulting utility work is handled safely by licensed contractors.

If the property is in a local historic district or the French Quarter, additional approvals may be required from the Historic Districts Landmarks Commission or Vieux Carré Commission. The city may also require City Planning approval depending on the project.

**Ask:** "Can I see the riggers insurance certificate, the permit application checklist, and the list of every separate permit and historic approval needed for my address?"

Ask whether the contractor's coverage applies to the lifting operation, temporary supports, property damage, and the actual subcontractors involved. Request current general liability and workers' compensation certificates as well. Have your insurer or broker confirm that the documents are appropriate for the project rather than relying on a certificate that simply says "insured."

## 5. Compare bids by the work included, not just the target height

A whole-house elevation estimate should explain the sequence and full scope. Compare at least two detailed proposals if practical. Each should identify:

- Surveying, engineering, plan preparation, permit fees, and inspections.
- Temporary shoring, lifting equipment, rigging, and the sequence for raising the structure.
- New foundation, piles, piers, connections, and structural repairs.
- Protection of walls, chimneys, porches, steps, and attached structures.
- Electrical, plumbing, gas, HVAC, and other utility disconnections and reconnections.
- New stairs, landings, ramps, porches, railings, and access improvements.
- Driveway, walkway, landscaping, grading, drainage, and site restoration.
- Debris removal, weather protection, temporary housing assumptions, and project schedule.
- Final inspections, elevation documentation, and permit closeout.

**Ask:** "What is excluded from this price, and what happens if the engineer finds concealed damage or the city requires a plan revision?"

The lowest bid may exclude major items that another contractor has included. Ask every bidder to identify allowances, unit prices, and change-order rates. Require written approval before extra work begins. Clarify who is responsible if the home is damaged during lifting or if the finished work does not match the approved drawings.

## 6. Put payments, schedule, and grant claims in writing

Use a signed contract that identifies the owner, the contracting company, the licensed raising contractor, the engineer, the scope, the price, payment milestones, schedule, insurance, warranties, and responsibility for permits and final approvals. Tie payments to verifiable progress such as completed engineering, approved permits, completed lifting, foundation work, utility reconnection, and final inspection. Avoid paying the full contract price before work is performed.

Be especially careful if a contractor says a grant will pay for the project. A historical Department of Justice case shows why grant paperwork and payment controls matter. In a July 12, 2017 press release, federal prosecutors said a New Orleans-area contractor pleaded guilty to false statements and theft of government funds connected to FEMA-funded house-elevation contracts. DOJ said the contractor had received advance funds but failed to perform work on at least three properties. This is a historical case, not evidence that current New Orleans contractors generally commit fraud. It is a reason to verify any grant, award, payment schedule, and contractor authorization directly with the agency before signing or transferring funds.

## 7. Make closeout documents part of the contract

A completed lift is not the same as a closed permit. Ask who will schedule required city inspections, obtain final approvals, provide updated elevation documentation, and resolve any failed inspection. Keep copies of the approved plans, permits, inspection results, benchmark certificate, final elevation certificate, utility permits, contractor invoices, and warranty documents.

**Ask:** "What exact documents will I receive before the final payment is due, and who is responsible for obtaining each one?"

The contract should also state how the contractor will correct work that does not match the engineered plans or pass inspection. Do not assume the contractor's warranty replaces city approval or the engineer's final documentation.

## Warning signs

- The contractor cannot identify the Louisiana license classification for the actual house-raising work.
- The contractor says a survey, engineered foundation drawings, or city permit is unnecessary.
- The company cannot provide a riggers insurance certificate for the lifting operation.
- The estimate omits utility work, stairs, foundation details, permits, or site restoration.
- The contractor promises a specific insurance discount or grant payment without written confirmation from the insurer or funding agency.
- A salesperson asks for a large advance payment before permits, plans, and project responsibilities are clear.
- The contractor will not identify the engineer, surveyor, or subcontractors.
- The company refuses to provide a written change-order process or final closeout requirements.

## Before you book

Confirm whether the project is whole-house elevation or settlement leveling. Verify the raising contractor's exact Louisiana license classification, get a licensed engineer and registered land surveyor involved, and compare complete written bids. Confirm the New Orleans elevation permit, riggers insurance, electrical and mechanical permits, and any historic-district approvals before work begins. Verify current requirements with the City of New Orleans and the Louisiana State Licensing Board for Contractors for your property and project.
